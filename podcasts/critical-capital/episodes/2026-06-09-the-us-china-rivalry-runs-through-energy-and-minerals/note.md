## The question

Can the United States and China keep competing without sliding into war?

## The answer

Allison's answer is that the historical base rate points to war, and he expects
the current order to keep unravelling rather than re-equilibrate. The thing
working against that outcome is entanglement rather than goodwill: the rare
earth export squeeze showed China able to stop American production lines within
a week or two, and Allison argues the dependence runs both ways, which makes
cooperation a condition of each country's own functioning.
On his account the hardest issues, Taiwan above all, are not resolvable but may
be manageable, as they have been for more than fifty years.

## The argument

The frame is the one Allison coined. When a rapidly rising power seriously
threatens to displace a ruling power, the normal outcome is war, often a
catastrophic one. What makes this more than an analogy is the mechanism he
attaches to it. The danger is not that either side chooses war; it is that the
rivalry magnifies misperception and multiplies miscalculation, so a small spark
can start a spiral nobody wanted. His example is the assassination in Sarajevo
in 1914, a tiny event that burned down Europe. His test for whether the
decisions were rational is retrospective: by 1918 every combatant except the
late-arriving United States had effectively been defeated, so given a do-over
none of those leaders would have chosen what they chose. He says both Xi and
Trump understand the present rivalry in these terms.

The second move is symmetry of grievance. China's story of itself runs from
being the center of the world for all of history to the last two hundred years,
in which Westerners with technology arrived to humiliate it, what the Chinese
call the century of humiliation. That makes the current project a rejuvenation
rather than an aggression. The American story is the "80, 80, and 9" record:
eighty years without great power war, eighty since nuclear weapons were used in
war, and nine nuclear-armed states rather than the twenty-five once forecast
for 1970, all of which Americans attribute to their own leadership and
guardianship of an international order. Allison's point is that each side's reading is
intelligible from inside the other's history, and that this buys nobody a pass.
It only means the motivations are more complex than a simple threat assessment,
and that if diplomacy as usual forecasts history as usual, the forecast is
catastrophe.

The turn in the conversation is the tariff fight and what it revealed. Allison
describes an escalation ladder in which tariffs went to 40, 60 and 100 percent
with Xi matching each move, and then to 145 percent, where Xi stopped at 125 and
did not match. That is the point at which the Treasury Secretary called it what
it was, an embargo. China then choked off the
supply of rare earth magnets and other items, and within a week or two American
production lines including the F-35 and the Ford Explorer were facing shutdown.
The American reversal was immediate, and Allison notes that Trump and Bessent
both described being caught out unusually candidly. The lesson he draws is not
that China won a round. It is that the two economies are entangled in each
other at a depth where each one's survival and wellbeing now requires some
cooperation from the other, which is much harder to act on than simple rivalry.
He reaches for the business term co-opetition and the example of Apple
depending on Samsung, its fiercest competitor, for critical iPhone components,
and Tim Cook's answer when pressed on it: life is complicated. The open
question he leaves is whether American strategists, and the American public,
can tolerate that much complexity. Taiwan is where it is most dangerous. He
believes, and believes Xi believes, that China would choose war over an
independent Taiwan even if the war were ultimately catastrophic for China,
because no Chinese leader could survive that outcome domestically. Against
that, the strategic ambiguity arrangement has held for the more than fifty
years since relations were re-established, which is his evidence that an
irresolvable issue is not necessarily an unmanageable one.

Energy enters mostly as a stress test rather than as a flashpoint, and this is
where Allison is most careful. He says he does not see a clear path from the
Iran war and the closure of the Strait of Hormuz to a US-China conflict, because
the two countries share an interest in reopening it, and he adds that maybe he
is not being imaginative enough. What the shock exposes instead is
uneven preparation. China was the only country making substantial additions to
its strategic petroleum reserve in January and February, before the attack on
Iran, and it has diversified into solar, wind and nuclear to the point that oil
is a small share of its energy consumption. Producers and net exporters are
fine. The pain lands on Japan, India, Indonesia, the Philippines and much of
Europe, and on Ukraine, since higher oil revenue funds Russia while the weapons
that might have gone to Kyiv are absorbed by the Middle East. Asked what it
would take to reach a stable hundred-year version of the 80-80-9 world, he
leads with the negative case and gives it the higher probability: an order under
assault by a wrecking ball, unravelling to some tragic outcome. His optimistic
case is not a plan but a precedent. The people who built the post-1945 order
were improvising daily rather than executing a design, and it has been renovated
repeatedly since, including the recovery of thousands of Soviet warheads after
1991. Renewal is possible in each arena, energy included, but he says it would
require imagination and initiative well beyond anything currently visible.

## What you need to know first

- **The Thucydides Trap.** Allison's term for the pattern the ancient historian
  described: a rising power frightens an established one, and the fear itself,
  rather than any decision to fight, makes war likely. The operative mechanism
  is misjudgment under pressure, not intent.
- **"80, 80, and 9."** The three numbers from a Foreign Affairs piece of his:
  years since the last great power war, years since nuclear weapons were used in
  war, and the number of states with nuclear arsenals. He uses them as the
  scoreboard for the order now under strain. The host coins "100, 100, 9" in a
  question, as shorthand for that order surviving another twenty years.
- **Rare earth magnets as a chokepoint.** Components most people had never
  thought about, concentrated enough in Chinese supply that export restrictions
  can stop unrelated American production lines within days. The leverage comes
  from the concentration, not from the value of the material.
- **Strategic ambiguity.** The Taiwan framework that came out of the Kissinger
  and Zhou Enlai talks. Allison describes it as China agreeing not to use force
  so long as red lines are not crossed, and the United States threatening to
  defend Taiwan if it is attacked.

## Details worth keeping

- The summit was heavily pre-negotiated. Allison credits Treasury Secretary
  Scott Bessent and his team working with their Chinese counterparts, whom the
  transcript renders as "He Lifeng, and the Vice Premier," which may be one
  person or two. He also credits back-channel work by the US Ambassador, David
  Perdue.
- One paragraph assessing the summit as not dramatic but very important, with
  the value lying in two leaders talking privately and candidly at length, is
  labelled in the transcript as the host but reads as a continuation of the
  guest's answer. Treat the attribution as uncertain.
- Allison says he thinks half the students in his class find a Martian invasion
  more likely than another great power war, and calls that shortsighted.
- He was involved in the Clinton administration effort to recover and eliminate
  the Soviet warheads left outside Russia after 1991, and says that without it
  9/11 could have been a nuclear 9/11.
- He calls the dollar more resilient than most would have forecast, while
  describing current US debt and deficit levels as clearly unsustainable and a
  genuine systemic financial risk.
- His energy optimism is unprompted and specific: that new technology lets him
  buy electricity more cheaply and reliably from solar than from oil, gas or
  coal, and that electric vehicles can beat internal combustion on price,
  reliability and emissions. On small nuclear modules he stays suspicious,
  saying he has seen the story several times before, while allowing that the
  investment now going into them may come to something valuable.

## Where it's contested

Nothing is contested. The host is a former student of the guest and says so in
the first minute, and the questions are admiring throughout. What follows is
what went untested and where the episode's framing runs ahead of the guest.

- **The title puts energy and minerals at the center; the guest does not.** Rare
  earths are the leverage that ends the escalation and his evidence that
  American exposure is structural, but they arrive inside an argument about
  entanglement rather than as a subject of their own. The oil shock appears as a
  shared American and Chinese interest, and he sees no clear path from it to a
  US-China conflict while allowing he may not be imaginative enough. The arena
  he names as most dangerous is Taiwan. He does once call energy the host's
  field rather than his, but answers that question substantively and returns to
  energy unprompted at the end.
- **The entanglement claim is asserted, not examined.** That mutual dependence
  now requires cooperation is the load-bearing hopeful element in the episode,
  and nobody asks what would happen if one side decided the dependence was worth
  breaking, or what reducing the rare earth dependence would actually take.
- **The Taiwan judgment is belief, stated as such.** Allison says he and Xi both
  believe China would go to war over Taiwanese independence even at catastrophic
  cost. That is a prediction about a decision nobody has had to make, and the
  host does not probe it.
- **The forecasts are offered as odds, not analysis.** The pessimistic scenario
  comes with betting language rather than an argument behind it, and a note that
  dropped that framing would make him sound far more certain than he is.
- **Host framing to watch.** The host supplies propositions inside his questions
  and the guest treats two of them differently. On Russia he amplifies rather
  than merely accepts: having earlier said only that the oil shock is not a
  problem for Russia, he answers by calling the situation about the best thing
  that could happen for Russia and the worst for Ukraine, and supplies the $150
  million a day himself. The description of passage through the Strait as
  partial is the host's alone; Allison speaks only of the embargo shutting in
  about 20% of global oil supply.
- **Transcript quality.** The closing voiceover is labelled "ALFRED TRACKING"
  rather than with a name, and is the host reading the guest's biography and the
  credits. One phrase renders Allison's own term as "lucidity and rivalries,"
  plainly a mangling of Thucydidean rivals.
