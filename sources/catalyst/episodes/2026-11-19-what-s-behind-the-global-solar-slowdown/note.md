## The question

Why will the world install less new solar in 2026 than in 2025, for the first
time on record, and what does that say about where solar goes next?

## The answer

Chase says the drop is real, the first since records begin in 1976, and that it
is not only a China story. It is saturation, which she says her team has
predicted for years: in many markets where solar is big, it floods the sunny
hours, which brings curtailment and low prices, makes new solar less attractive
and leads governments to plan for less. Batteries ease the effect and keep solar
being built, but do not restart growth or completely reverse the feedback;
along with government auctions, they are how she accounts, using European
examples, for the roughly flat installation Kann reads in her firm's forecast
for Europe and North America.

## The argument

Predicting a down year for global solar has been, as Kann puts it, a fool's
errand for many years, and he offers Chase a last chance to retract hers. She
declines: it is November, there will be no late reversal, and installations
fall for the first time in records going back to 1976. Kann suggests it is
predominantly a China story. Chase says it is not only that. China is more than
half the world market, so whatever happens there is a big part of the global
story, but the underlying cause is a negative feedback loop. So much solar now
generates in the sunny hours, in much of China and elsewhere, that output gets
curtailed and spot power prices in those hours fall. That makes new solar less
attractive to build, and it leads policymakers to set policy for less of it. The
big change in China, a mid-2025 rule forcing renewable developers to sell on
spot markets instead of at a fixed price, she describes as partly a response to
that saturation. She calls the effect global, a big deal from Brazil to China,
with much of Europe now seeing very low prices in sunny hours, and says it is
not going away.

The obvious objection is batteries, which are scaling quickly, and Kann puts it.
Chase's reply is that batteries do ease the problem but do not kickstart growth.
Australia and Chile, both of which added many batteries this year, are seeing
less curtailment. But Chile's was very high to begin with, so even if it is
down slightly, a lot of solar is still being dumped, and that remains a strong
reason not to build. And batteries compete with each other before they help
solar. Historically they earned much of their money selling grid services that
handle tiny fluctuations, not shifting bulk energy.
The first batteries in Australia that did shift midday solar into the evening
earned well, but as more arrived they pulled the evening price down much more
than they lifted the midday price. Her summary is that "batteries eat batteries
before they help solar"; the help is real but "relatively small as yet." A
storage boom, which she says is "sort of" under way, keeps solar being built
without completely reversing the feedback.

Kann reads her team's published forecast as a dip followed by renewed global
growth at much more incremental rates, with roughly flat annual installation in
Europe and North America for five to seven years, "something like that." His
own gloss is that mature markets plateau while batteries keep installations
from collapsing, and markets that do not yet have much solar carry global
growth. To the flat Western outlook, and his question whether it comes down to
batteries combating curtailment, Chase says yes and names two effects, with
European examples. Batteries are one, sometimes simply by being co-located with
the panels, particularly on homes. The other is government auctions in Europe
that keep buying solar and wind even where market prices do not necessarily
support it, because renewable targets aim to push gas out of the mix and make
power cheaper overall. His market-by-market account of global growth she meets
with a qualifying "well": rather than restate the forecast, she stresses how
much of the world's population already lives in somewhat solarized countries
such as China and India. Large populations remain (Indonesia, which she is
"waiting to really break through," much of Southeast Asia, Bangladesh,
Africa), but even as module sales into Africa rise, she
expects them to take a long time to reach substantial volumes, because buyers
there are very poor and solar is a lot for them even though it is cheap. When
Kann closes by saying the down year should not be an overly negative takeaway,
since it will still be well over half a terawatt, she replies that half a
terawatt in a year is a lot.

Saturation also has a seasonal edge that matters most in the north. Her own
house in Switzerland can produce 60 kilowatt-hours a day from its solar in
summer and 3 in November, when it also needs more power for heating; with
a big battery it bought almost no grid power from spring to September, and now
buys nearly all of it. A battery that charges and discharges only once a year
cannot pay for itself however cheap it is, which she gives as one reason
Northern Europe has to build wind: something clean has to charge the batteries
in winter, and whether it is wind or nuclear "doesn't matter to me." Almost
any energy-system model, except perhaps India's and parts of the Middle East,
asks for wind for a net-zero system, because wind blows when solar is weak.
The danger she flags is a path of building only solar and storage to roughly
90% dependence on them, at which point wind has no case
for the remaining tenth of the year, gas fills it, and the system stays locked
into gas at relatively high levels. Building wind alongside solar, she argues,
probably makes the last stretch a lot easier.

## What you need to know first

- **Curtailment.** Solar output that is produced but thrown away because the
  grid cannot use it or it is not worth selling. High curtailment is both a
  symptom of saturation and a reason not to build more.
- **Spot market versus fixed price.** Selling power at whatever the market pays
  hour by hour, rather than at a price agreed in advance.
- **Grid services versus bulk arbitrage.** Batteries can earn money either by
  helping the grid operator handle tiny, fast fluctuations (she names frequency
  response and black start) or by buying cheap midday power and selling it in
  the evening. Only the second directly helps solar.

## Details worth keeping

- **Balcony solar is "a bit overhyped," in her view.** A single plug-in module
  can run a fridge most of the time and little more, whereas a proper rooftop
  system can charge a car, a battery and sometimes a heat pump. She says many of
  the panels she sees are broken, covered or badly installed, which is probably
  not great advocacy for solar. Kann explains the enthusiasm as an extreme form
  of residential solar's self-determination appeal; Chase grants the
  "feel-good factor" but answers that going off grid is expensive, "we have a
  power grid for a reason," and power is not meant to be exciting. She has
  nothing against it and does not think it changes the trajectory of the
  transition.
- **The US, in her telling, lags.** California and Texas are doing "pretty
  okay," but she says overall US penetration and forecasts lag, which is slow
  for so rich and sunny a country. Kann supplies "China specifically" and asks
  about Europe; she answers Europe and Latin America. US solar is at once
  unusually profitable, because of layered tax credits, and
  unusually expensive. She jokes that someone is making a lot of money, "it
  seems to be lawyers," and says the US transition is slower and costlier than
  it could be.
- **Pakistan's boom followed expensive, unreliable grid power.** Around 2022
  the government and the "discos" (her term, not spelled out) let power prices
  rise, where a lot of other similarly poor countries subsidized power to keep
  prices low; power was also unreliable and people "really hate" their utility
  companies. Homes, small businesses and factories installed solar, in many
  cases by people who had watched a TikTok video on how to do it (local-language
  installation TikTok is, she says, apparently huge there), and solar has
  replaced diesel for irrigation on a massive scale, which is depleting
  groundwater in various areas because farmers pump more when they do not pay
  for it. She says Pakistan is one of very few countries "in Southeast Asia"
  growing economically without increasing gas imports, and that demand has
  dropped noticeably for incumbent generators. She thinks it is probably
  slowing a little.
- **In the Philippines, a lot of its solar is already co-located with
  storage**, which she says is a first; in earlier waves elsewhere, solar
  without storage went first, sometimes with some storage later. "We'll see how
  that goes."
- **Bulgaria is building many batteries**, partly through a fund under the
  European Emissions Trading Scheme that pays for infrastructure in the poorer
  Eastern European members, and with strong links to its neighbors it aims to
  act as a regional hub, soaking up their solar and, she says, hopefully making
  money by letting it out later.
- **South Africa's boom has receded.** Households that were not particularly
  poor bought solar during years of rolling blackouts from aging coal plants;
  once the plants were maintained and more solar arrived, the grid grew more
  reliable and the market shrank. Chinese exports to Nigeria, Kenya and most of
  Sub-Saharan Africa are rising from a small base, and a bunch of gold and
  copper mines across the region now have solar and batteries instead of
  diesel, though she says information from the ground is hard to get.
- **Europe is still building wind**, with a lot of offshore in Germany. She
  describes wind as a stable sector whose costs and installations change little
  year to year and whose reliance on offtake contracts gives good visibility on
  pipelines. Kann contrasts the US, where he says the current administration has
  targeted wind above all other sources.
- **Southern Europe is further north than it feels.** Southern Spain is only at
  roughly New York's latitude and still has heating demand, so winter power
  demand is probably higher there too. It is probably much easier to run on
  solar than Switzerland or the Nordics, but she says you want wind there as
  well.

## Where it's contested

- **China's role.** Kann frames the down year as predominantly a China story;
  Chase says it is "not only" that. She grants China is a big part of the
  global story and calls its 2025 policy change "the big change" there, but
  describes that change as partly a response to the same global saturation.
- **Tariffs and the US lag.** The link between tariffs and the US falling behind
  is Kann's question, not Chase's claim. She opens her answer with "yeah," thin
  assent at most, then dates the lag to 2012 without explaining it, keeps to it
  under pushback, and later points to high costs alongside generous incentives
  rather than to tariffs by name. On module quality the two disagree outright:
  Kann calls the cheaper modules "very, very similar," Chase says they are
  better, Kann allows "maybe," and she repeats "I know they're better."
- **The forecast beyond 2026 is Kann's reading** of BloombergNEF's published
  numbers, not something Chase states; see the argument for how she answers it.
- **Hedges.** Beyond the "I think" figures in Claims, she is unsure of the US
  incentive details ("some kind of unionized labor I think"), says data from
  Pakistan and Sub-Saharan Africa is poor, and offers the 90% solar-and-storage
  path as a risk to avoid, not a forecast.
- **What went unexamined.** The down-year call is her own team's forecast and
  she defends it with confidence; beyond inviting a retraction, Kann does not
  probe the numbers behind it.
