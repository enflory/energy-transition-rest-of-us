## The question

With the three big gas turbine makers sold out into the 2030s, who is rushing in
to fill the gap, and which of the newcomers are likely to last?

## The answer

Brough expects the big three to stay dominant and the market to stay healthy
for a decade, even as orders start declining after about 2028. Among the
newcomers he rates jet-engine conversions and supercritical carbon dioxide
systems as viable, and doubts that the from-scratch turbine designers Kann names
(Boom Supersonic, Parallax) will succeed, calling it a potential 10- to 20-year
effort. He expects fleets of small reciprocating engines to be the first thing
buyers abandon once the rush eases, because they are a logistics problem at data
center scale, and sees linear generators as a similar long-term reach.

## The argument

The starting point is that the established makers are more sold out than before.
Brough is tracking 2026 orders at or above his own upside scenario, and a new
reservation with GE Vernova, Siemens Energy or Mitsubishi today means delivery
in 2030 to 2032. They are adding factory capacity, but deliberately not
overbuilding, because by his account they remain leery of an AI bubble bursting.
Even after expansion, each of the three projects capacity of roughly 23 to 30
gigawatts a year by 2030, while every other maker is at 5 gigawatts or less.
Kann sets that against data center forecasts for the US alone and concludes the
big three cannot meet demand by the end of the decade if the boom continues.
Brough answers "I think that's true," adds his own North American demand figure,
and notes that the hole is being filled from many directions, with some of the
dynamics behind it striking him as not common sense.

His test for the newcomers is how hard the engineering is and how the units
behave at scale. Designing a gas turbine from the ground up is the hardest
path: he calls the machine a "symphony in metal," notes that Boom Supersonic
"basically lost" its largest order, doubts Parallax's 10-megawatt unit will
soon be a real turbine, and puts such efforts at potentially 10 to 20 years,
while allowing that they might surprise him. Converting used aircraft engines
for stationary power is different. In his view there are only two major
challenges (removing the front fan designed to produce thrust so the engine
delivers rotating torque instead, and reworking combustion away from liquid
fuel), and the feedstock is large, so he expects
those companies to succeed. Supercritical carbon dioxide cycles also clear his
bar, because they lean on technology closer to steam turbines, though he
expects only a small market impact.

Small units turn on scale rather than engineering. Kann offers the strongest
case for them: in bridge power, where a site runs almost entirely on its own
generation, many small modules make it easier to carry spare capacity than
adding 50-megawatt blocks. Brough calls that "absolutely correct" under today's
lead times, but argues that hundreds of small machines on one site are a
logistics and maintenance nightmare, so when demand diminishes for any reason,
from recession to regulation to data center failures, those orders will vanish
first. He applies the same objection to linear generators on footprint grounds.

The economic picture follows from scarcity. Mid-size units now sell for several
times what they did not long ago, which has flipped the makers' business model:
for the first time, the largest ones earn more on new units than on servicing
old ones. Brough expects prices to fall but settle well above where they were,
which he thinks is good for the industry. And the long view is a decline, not a
collapse. Renewables are still being added at an order of magnitude more
capacity per year than gas turbines and are, in his view, the cheapest
electricity globally including in the US, which will pull gas turbine orders
down; but even his worst case keeps orders ten years out above 2023 levels.

## What you need to know first

- **The big three.** GE Vernova, Siemens Energy and Mitsubishi, the established
  turbine manufacturers the show refers to as OEMs, which dominate supply.
- **Slot reservation.** A paid hold on a future production slot, short of a
  firm order. Brough says GE Vernova is the only maker that reports these
  separately from firm orders.
- **Aeroderivative.** A gas turbine adapted from an aircraft engine design. The
  newer variant discussed here takes actual used jet engines off aircraft and
  converts them.
- **Bridge power.** Onsite generation that carries a data center, almost
  entirely on its own, for the period before it has a grid connection.

## Details worth keeping

- Popular turbine sizes for data centers, with a lot of order activity: 14 to 17
  megawatts (Siemens Energy's SGT-400, Baker Hughes's NovaLT16, Solar's Titan
  130); 25 to 35 megawatts, mostly aeroderivatives (LM2500, Siemens SGT-A35,
  MHI's FT8); and growing interest above 40 megawatts (LM6000, TP6000, LM9000).
  The largest orders, which he attributes to Elon Musk, were 300-plus megawatt
  units.
- Jet-engine converters named: the transcript renders the best-known as
  "F-TI" (the show notes list "FTAI"), plus Nova Power and, per Brough, at least
  one to three others not yet public.
- ProEnergy is a distinct variant: it buys an aircraft engine but keeps only the
  outer casing, designing and making nearly all the internals itself, with
  "some degree of success."
- Kann's framing of the moment: a "let a thousand flowers bloom" (or "sign
  POs") phase, with behind-the-meter demand predicated almost entirely on
  getting online faster.

## Claims worth citing

All as stated on 2026-10-29. Order books, lead times and prices in this market
move quickly.

- Gas turbine orders in 2026 expected at 126 to 132 gigawatts, against his
  earlier prediction of about 101; 126 was his upside scenario. (Brough)
- GE Vernova holds about 53 gigawatts of firm orders plus about 63 gigawatts of
  slot reservations, "almost at 120" combined, roughly equal to Siemens Energy
  and Mitsubishi together. Siemens Energy backlog about 70 gigawatts,
  Mitsubishi about 35, "to the best of my reckoning." (Brough)
- Some makers have told him certain product lines are sold out for the next
  five years. (Brough)
- Annual capacity by 2030: GE from about 20 to 30 gigawatts, Siemens from about
  16 to 23, Mitsubishi almost doubling. Beyond the big three, Baker Hughes is
  roughly doubling, Solar Turbines almost doubling, and Doosan entering as a new
  player. (Brough) Kann's rough total of 75 gigawatts a year for the three is
  his own arithmetic.
- North American data center capacity demand of about 150 to 160 gigawatts by
  2030. (Brough)
- Data center turbines purchased today mostly run 7 to over 100 megawatts;
  the small engines at issue are under 3 megawatts. (Brough)
- Sites planning "between 5 and 800" small reciprocating engines each, as
  rendered; he later refers to 500 to 600 devices, so the lower bound is likely
  garbled. (Brough)
- A Mainspring string of 10 linear generators gives 2.5 megawatts on about
  6,000 square feet, where 30 to 100 megawatts of turbines could fit. (Brough)
- Units of 20 to 35 megawatts sell for $1,900 to $2,500 per kilowatt, versus
  about $400 to $500 not long ago. He later states the current range as $1,900
  to $2,200. (Brough)
- He expects prices to settle around $800 to $900 per kilowatt, still double
  what makers in that size range expected five years ago. (Brough)
- Tens of thousands of aircraft engines could become available for conversion
  over the next 5 to 10 years. (Brough)

## Where it's contested

- **Small modular units.** Kann puts forward an argument he has heard, that they
  make sense regardless of market conditions for reliability reasons; Brough
  accepts that for now but holds that they will be first to go.
- **Novel technologies.** Kann groups Mainspring's linear generators with fuel
  cells such as Bloom. Brough grants "some viability" to the category, but his
  specific footprint objection is to Mainspring; he does not address Bloom
  directly.
- **The big three's capacity.** Kann restates Mitsubishi and Siemens as being
  in a 10 to 20 gigawatt range; Brough's own figures put all three at 23 to 30
  by 2030.
- **Forecasts, labeled as such.** The 2028 turn, the price settling point and
  the success or failure of each newcomer are Brough's predictions, and he
  hedges the de novo verdict ("maybe they'll surprise me").
- **Date of the prior conversation.** Kann's monologue places Brough's last
  appearance in June 2023, while in conversation he says it was a year to a
  year and a half ago. The transcript does not resolve it.
