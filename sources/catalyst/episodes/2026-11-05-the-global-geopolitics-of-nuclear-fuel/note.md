## The question

Uranium is at a record price, yet the utilities that buy it are calm. Will the
uranium actually be there?

## The answer

Alkin says no: there is not enough *economically viable* uranium, because
utilities spent a decade buying too little, at prices too low to pay for new
mines, while the cheapest producer's costs climbed. Conversion and enrichment
are tight as well, but they are services that higher prices are already
expanding within a few years, whereas a mine can take decades, so he places the
constraint upstream in mining. Geopolitics, which the title leads with, enters
mostly as the concentration of supply in the East; on the Russian ban, his
point is that lifting it would not flood the market, because Russia is short
of uranium itself.

## The argument

The buyers' calm has real roots, and Alkin lays them out before arguing that
they mislead. Uranium has spent most of its history in a bear market: more has
been mined since the 1940s than consumed, and each major accident (Three Mile
Island, Chernobyl, Fukushima) had a lingering psychological effect that pulled
demand back and let surplus supply depress prices. For a reactor owner the fuel
is both irreplaceable and cheap. There is nothing to switch to, the way a
utility might shift between its coal and gas plants, yet uranium itself is,
by his rough arithmetic, only a high-single to low-double-digit share of a
reactor's operating cost. So buyers put security of supply ahead of price and
lock it in with contracts of five to twelve years or longer. The people doing
the buying are nuclear engineers rewarded for never running out of fuel, not
traders rewarded for buying low, so they have no incentive to call bottoms.
Because most uranium is bought on long contracts, he says, recency bias and
complacency seep in, and the market's opacity sustains them: the "uranium
fairy," as an industry saying has it, always delivers.

The price signal that might shake that complacency is weak by construction.
There is no exchange. The spot market, a fifth to a quarter of volume, is
dominated by traders and by producers squaring their own books, yet as the only
daily price it is the one everyone watches. The monthly long-term price is
narrower still. Price reporters base it on what Alkin calls base-escalated
contracts, a minority of term volume, and record the lowest offer they find
rather than the price actually struck. Most uranium is sold in bilateral deals
that never go out to tender. Through 2021 to 2023, he says, buyers used the
tenders they did issue to draw low bids from small developers with little
chance of ever producing, knowing those bids would hold the reported price
down. His conclusion is that the published numbers are not where the market
trades, which is the caveat Kann attaches to his own opening record price.

Underneath the calm is the reversal. Over the past decade utilities' replacement
rate, the share of what they consume each year that they sign new contracts to
cover, has been about 55%, with the rest drawn from inventory. So buyers have
not yet tested what it costs to bring forth new production. When Alkin began
modeling every reactor and mine in 2016, with prices in the high teens, he
concluded that pulling uranium out of the ground needed well over $50 a pound
once legacy contracts ran off. Prices have since risen about fivefold anyway,
without supply ever being stressed. Meanwhile the cost floor moved.
Kazatomprom, Kazakhstan's state producer and by far the world's cheapest,
projected costs of $13 to $14 a pound for 2025 when it listed and is instead
at about $40, and production has been cut back across the industry. Hence his
verdict that "that horse has left the barn": new mines are coming online, but
they do not cover the gap.

Why mining rather than the middle of the chain? "Price is the great elixir,"
he says, but it works on different clocks. Uranium is the fuel cycle's only
product; conversion, enrichment and fabrication are services, and conversion
and enrichment prices have risen steeply enough to pull in new capacity within
a few years. A mine can take 20 to 25 years from discovery to production,
junior miners' low-cost claims are not credible when the cheapest producer is
at $40, and inventories get dearer as prices rise rather than more mobile.
Tight enrichment even feeds back onto uranium. In the post-Fukushima glut,
enrichers with spare capacity "underfed," producing surplus uranium to sell,
at one point a volume he equates to the world's largest mine. With enrichment
now short, he expects the reverse, "overfeeding," which turns enrichers into
buyers of uranium, while saying plainly that this has not happened yet.
Russia fits the same frame. The expectation that a Ukraine
settlement or an end to the 2028 ban would let Russia flood the market misreads
it: Russia needs uranium for its own reactors and for those it builds and fuels
abroad, and is scrambling for supply. Lifting the ban would mean more Russian
enriched uranium, he allows, but not more uranium, and enrichment prices have
already risen and contracts are being signed.

## What you need to know first

- **The fuel cycle.** Mined uranium is processed into a powder called
  yellowcake, converted into a gas (uranium hexafluoride), spun in centrifuges
  to raise its fissile share (the part that sustains the chain reaction) from
  the 0.7% it has naturally to the 3% to 5% power reactors need, then
  fabricated into pellets loaded into tubes. The steps after mining take 18 to
  24 months, depending on available capacity.
- **Spot versus term price.** Spot is a one-off purchase, reported daily. The
  term price is reported monthly and is based on base-escalated contracts: a
  starting price fixed at today's quoted level, escalating by an agreed rate
  (his example is 3% a year), with deliveries beginning a few years out.
- **Underfeeding and overfeeding.** An enricher can trade uranium against
  centrifuge time. With spare capacity, it keeps centrifuges spinning on a
  customer's uranium to extract more than the order needs, and sells the
  surplus. With capacity short, it does the reverse, putting in extra uranium
  to fill orders in less time, which makes it a buyer.

## Details worth keeping

- Supply splits by ownership as well as geography. Eastern production and
  enrichment are largely state-owned. Western enrichment, he says, is owned by
  private companies or the state; his examples are France's state-owned Orano
  and Urenco, a Dutch, English and German consortium he describes as
  state-owned alongside a couple of small German utilities, and notes is not
  public. China has little domestic uranium but significant
  production in Namibia.
- The spot market is a traders' market. Japanese trading houses, banks, one-off
  traders and now large oil and gas traders such as Mercuria take uncommitted
  material, sometimes as offtake from mines they helped finance or from
  byproduct streams like BHP's Olympic Dam copper mine in South Australia, and
  trade it back and forth for 50 cents to a dollar a pound. Even for the big
  trading houses this is not a large business.
- A utility buying spot is a warning sign: processing takes years, so spot
  material cannot reach its reactor in time anyway.
- His analogy for term-price reporting: a house sells for a million dollars,
  someone had offered $800,000, and the $800,000 is what gets reported.
- In 2016 nuclear conferences had no Wall Street presence, prices and uranium
  equities had collapsed 95% by his account, the sell side had stopped
  modeling the sector, and fuel buyers told him prices were headed to $10 or
  $5.
- Kazatomprom's costs are in tenge and its sales in dollars, so a weaker tenge
  helps its profitability. The Kazakhs, he says, have dropped the Soviet-style
  habit of producing more while losing money.
- Russia's reactor exports, which he places across the Belt and Road, come
  with Russian help on financing and operation, and with Russian fuel. The US
  has lately sent back very little of the uranium feed (he calls it U3O8) that
  it returns in exchange for Russian enriched uranium. Russia does underfeed
  its own centrifuges, he says, "but it's a challenge for them."

## Where it's contested

- **Almost no pushback.** Kann's questions probe how the market works, such as
  why a spot market exists at all, but he does not challenge the thesis, so it
  rests on one speaker's account. Load-bearing points nobody tested: what price
  would make enough uranium "economically viable" today (the only threshold
  given is his 2016 model's); how large the drawn-down inventories are
  ("staggering," never sized); how far short the new mines fall; and why
  Kazatomprom's costs ran so far past projection. The 55% replacement rate and
  the portrait of complacent buyers come from Alkin alone, with no fuel buyer
  in the conversation.
- **The prices do not reconcile** (see the first three Claims). That fits his
  argument that reported prices are not where the market trades, but he draws
  that connection only through a hypothetical (a contract struck at $102 to
  $105 while an offer of $97 is what gets reported), never by reconciling the
  episode's own figures. Nor does he spell out what he means when he
  says that in today's seller's market "the opposite is happening" to the
  buyers' tender tactic.
- **Mining as "the" bottleneck is Kann's wording.** Alkin argues toward it, but
  his own sentence beginning "the bottleneck is going to be" trails off
  unfinished, and he says conversion still needs capacity and enrichment is
  short "right now."
- **The monologue's framing is not the guest's.** Kann opens on supply chains
  running through Russia, Kazakhstan and China, and on fuel for the new
  reactors "we are now promising to build." Alkin's case rests instead on
  contracting, price reporting and production cost. He mentions demand growth
  only in passing, and new reactors only as Russia's export business, never the
  build-out Kann refers to. He calls the Russian ban "important" but treats it
  as settled, without describing what happens once it takes effect.
- **The guest's vantage point.** Alkin describes 30 years in hedge funds
  looking at out-of-favor industries, and coming to uranium in 2016, managing
  his own money, then concluding after two years of modeling that the price was
  below the cost of production; a sentence about "my vehicle" is cut off. The
  episode does not say what positions his firm holds, and Kann does not raise
  it.
