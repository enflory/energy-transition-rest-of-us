## The question

What does it actually take to get a new nuclear plant in the US from an empty
site to a plant that runs?

## The answer

In Faherty's telling, the hard part is less the reactor than the business
model, capital formation and risk allocation around it, so many of his choices
turn on whether they make the project financeable by cautious capital. That
means a site chosen around a customer, a long contract with a creditworthy
buyer, a mature light-water reactor with a working fuel supply chain, an
experienced utility as operator, money raised in tranches that each retire a
specific risk, a finished design before any concrete is poured, and a
portfolio of standardized projects so that repetition, above all at a single
site, can bring costs down.

## The argument

Kann opens with a thesis he presents as his own and that of his firm, Energy
Impact Partners (EIP): a better reactor, even a much better one, does not by
itself create a project, and while gas, wind, solar and storage all have a
developer who finds the site, signs the customer, gets through the grid queue
and the regulator, assembles the vendors and raises the money, nuclear mostly
does not. He says this gap "might be" the bigger bottleneck. Faherty puts it in
his own terms. Nuclear, he says, does not have a technology problem "as
much as" it has a business model, capital formation and risk allocation
problem, and he stays "very open" to the newer "Gen IV" designs; his point is
that de-risking technology is not the job of Elementl (rendered "Elemental" in
the spoken transcript) and not what its investors expect of it. When Kann sums
up the approach as framing many decisions, reactor choice among them, around
whether the project can be financed, Faherty answers "It absolutely is" and
adds insurers to the list of people it must satisfy. His sequence starts from
the customer rather than the land: where a buyer needs power and will pay a
bit more for early projects that are still learning, and only then which sites
work, which is why Kann remarks that they might have taken his list in
reverse.

The financing logic runs as a chain. Debt requires an offtake contract with an
investment-grade buyer, for as long a term as possible, whether the lender is a
public one (rendered "EDF" in the transcript, formerly, he says, the Loan
Programs Office) or, he hopes, private markets. Kann characterizes the buyers
as hyperscalers and "the AI complex"; Faherty answers "It is" and immediately
pivots to what matters for finance, which is creditworthiness and tenor.
Lenders also need a credible capacity factor, since that defines the cash flow,
and it depends on both the operator and the technology. Hence Elementl, as
things stand today, will not operate plants itself but partner with utilities
that have fleets, training programs and standing with the regulator. Hence
also the reactor: GE Vernova Hitachi's BWRX-300, which he describes as the
tenth generation of a boiling-water design, with fewer components than a
pressurized-water reactor, passive safety, a unit already under construction
at Darlington, and fuel already being fabricated, though he says new builds
will still need additional supply chain investment. A newer vendor must design
and license its reactor and may have to stand up significant parts of the
supply chain, and for some Gen IV designs insurers still need educating on
products and pricing. That matters because insurance is needed to get debt,
and debt to get equity; light-water designs and their vendors are already
known quantities to insurers. He concedes the trade-off: the large AP1000
probably reaches a lower lifetime cost per unit of electricity than the
smaller reactors, but fewer sites can host it.

Capital is raised in stages, each tied to milestones that retire risk so the
next tranche comes cheaper. Venture money has poured into new reactor
technologies and, he thinks, funding across climate tech tends to be
plentiful for the "widget," but project investors, unlike venture investors,
have no shot at a hundredfold payout, so they are far more conservative,
above all early on, when risk is greatest. Development money, spent from
inception to the final investment decision, costs something "akin to
venture." Past that decision the asset's
value steps up and construction equity is cheaper, and an operating
light-water plant carries a utility-like cost of capital. Elementl works
backward from the operating plant's value to what a developer can earn by
getting a project to that decision. Its answer to the expensive first stage is
a development capital facility, so far only a preliminary term sheet, in which
investors would be repaid on the performance of a portfolio of projects, so
that if one project were paused or killed they could still earn a return on
later ones.
Sometimes, he says, to solve a big problem "you just have to make it bigger,"
and the same portfolio gives customers a credible pipeline, suppliers multiple
chances to participate, operators a fleet rather than a one-off plant, and
financiers a process they can standardize.

Construction and repetition close the loop. The lesson Faherty takes from
Vogtle is to complete the design before breaking ground and to bring the
constructor and the licensing experts in during design, so the license does not
specify things a field crew cannot achieve. A finished design permits a cost
estimate built on paid, underwritable supplier quotes, work he says has not
always been incentivized before ground is broken. Operator training and
workforce recruiting have to start early rather than wait for groundbreaking,
with enough money in the contract and financing to retain people throughout.
On the learning curve, the nuclear island is only part of a project, so
standardization has to extend to the balance of plant and the balance of site,
and an integrator working across many projects can enforce it in a way that is
hard when projects are sold to individual utilities, whose nuclear chiefs may
each want different equipment (he cites valve types). He expects the largest
gains from repeating at one location, as the local workforce and supply chain
learn the technology, and sees standardized procurement of long-lead
materials, on the model of a federal supply-chain loan program, as replicable
for other designs once they have a large enough order book of credible
projects and a standardized technology whose risk has been retired, so lenders
can treat the assets as interchangeable across projects.

## What you need to know first

- **Offtake contract with an investment-grade counterparty.** A long-term
  agreement to buy the plant's output, signed by a buyer whose credit lenders
  trust.
- **Final investment decision.** The point at which debt and equity are
  committed and construction can begin. Faherty sometimes calls it the
  "financial investment decision"; by context it is the same milestone.
- **Capacity factor.** A plant's actual output over a year as a share of what
  it would produce running flat out the whole time.
- **Nuclear island, balance of plant, balance of site.** The reactor and its
  directly associated systems; the rest of the power plant; and the site works
  around both.

## Details worth keeping

- Site screening sorts criteria into exclusions that kill a site outright (such
  as seismic risk or a community that does not want the project) and
  "avoidance" criteria that need deeper study of cost and scope (soil, bedrock,
  logistics, distance to transmission). The co-founders' first attempt, to buy
  a retiring coal site on a river with existing transmission, turned up
  problems in diligence with buildable acreage, the floodplain and coal ash
  liabilities. Elementl then worked with Oak Ridge National Laboratory and its
  screening tool, OR-SAGE, and has since built an internal tool that screens an
  area almost live from a circle drawn on a map. A site also has to be
  transactable: owners may not sell, and a utility may treat a good site as a
  "crown jewel."
- Community engagement is phased. Elementl draws on polling work by an adviser,
  Todd Allen of the University of Michigan, and in Ohio engaged local officials
  up through the State House before any announcement. That site had previously
  been sited for a coal plant, so the community's openness to a large plant and
  its jobs was already known.
- Kann notes that interconnection is surprisingly hard for nuclear even though
  its long lead time probably makes a long queue tolerable; Faherty calls it
  "tricky," needing technical knowledge of the connection point. Elementl
  commissioned preliminary studies of the required upgrades, and tracks the
  regional grid operator's process (PJM, not spelled out on air) closely. It
  deliberately avoids stacking other generation at the same connection point,
  despite reasons to do so he calls "pretty compelling"; perhaps later, he
  says, once Elementl, regulators and other stakeholders have more experience.
- Region follows the difficulty of building alternatives. Texas (no capacity
  market, plentiful gas, wind and solar, generally easier to build) has not
  been a core market, and he says the country west of the Mississippi shares
  many of those attributes; east of it, solar and wind can be harder to build
  and some areas lack gas pipeline capacity. That led to a first project
  in the Northeast and Midwest.
- The offtake model borrows from LNG projects, where creditworthy buyers put
  money in early alongside a solid contract. Customers,
  he says, want an "easy button": sign a power purchase agreement and trust the
  team to deliver.
- Two construction-era anecdotes. At Vogtle, license specifications fixed rebar
  spacing so precisely that daily heating and cooling moved it out of license
  tolerance while it stayed within engineering tolerance, and he says poor
  coordination among licensing, constructor and vendor led to "really
  unfortunate decisions." At Barakah (which he names but does not locate), the
  initial plants saw some delays because operator training had not started
  early enough.
- Workforce strategy runs through trade schools, colleges and trade
  organizations, plus a long-term manning program that moves experienced
  workers from one site to the next. He argues a pipeline of projects makes
  this more attractive to partners, because it offers a career rather than one
  job.
- Faherty and his co-founders operated light-water reactors in the Navy; he
  served on the submarine USS Memphis.

## Claims worth citing

All figures as stated on 2026-11-12 and attributed to the speaker, not verified
independently. Return and cost-of-capital figures move with interest rates.

- A 20 to 25 year creditworthy offtake is what debt requires today. (Faherty)
- Development capital, inception to final investment decision, costs something
  akin to venture: returns of 20% to 30% a year and 3 to 5 times the money
  invested; he also calls it "25-plus percent." (Faherty)
- Construction equity after that decision: roughly 11% unlevered, perhaps 20%
  levered. (Faherty)
- An operating light-water plant: a cost of capital around 10%. He points to
  Constellation's weighted average cost of capital and says Constellation,
  along with other utilities, could be a natural owner. (Faherty)
- Elementl values a finished plant assuming a 92% capacity factor. This is a
  valuation assumption, not a measured result. (Faherty)
- Designing and licensing a new reactor costs about $1 billion, before any
  supply chain build-out. (Faherty)
- The nuclear island is "approximately one third of most projects." He does not
  say one third of what, cost or scope. (Faherty)
- Vogtle trained five to six technicians for every one out in the field, and
  had "a real attrition problem." The phrasing is compressed; read the
  transcript before quoting. (Faherty)
- Site screening that once took six months and several specialists now runs
  nearly live. (Faherty)
- The facility so far rests on a preliminary agreement (a term sheet) with DSIP,
  which he describes as a Korean financial institution with deep relationships
  with Korean "strategics," and Samsung C&T, which he says represents key
  members of the nuclear supply chain. It offers investors preferred returns;
  more participants are hoped for within one to two quarters; the goal is to
  fund most development for three grid-scale projects. (Faherty)
- In Ohio the intention is to go from one to two to five at the same location,
  with "very reasonable expectations" of clear steps down the cost curve. No
  cost figures are given. (Faherty)
- A project like the one in Ohio represents hundreds of long-term jobs.
  (Faherty)
- A three to five year interconnection wait is probably acceptable for nuclear,
  given its lead time. (Kann)

## Where it's contested

- **A portfolio company conversation**, disclosed up front as is routine on this
  show; the facility, the Ohio plan, the cost-curve expectation and the 92%
  assumption are Elementl's plans and targets, and the nuclear utilities Faherty
  praises as operators include, he notes in passing, investors in EIP.
- **Little pushback, one clarification.** Kann mostly builds on Faherty's
  answers rather than testing them. His one clarification is that the federal
  supply-chain loan model Faherty praises covers only the AP1000, which Faherty
  confirms. That is not the reactor Elementl chose, and Faherty's claim that the
  model is replicable for others carries the conditions noted above.
- **Claims that are Kann's alone.** Several framings come from the host and
  were not argued by the guest: that the customers are the AI complex (brief
  assent only, as above); that permitting looks better than it has in a long
  time (never discussed with Faherty); that nuclear projects have "died on the
  interconnection vine" (Faherty says only that the process is tricky); that
  utilities will not take early risk and reactor vendors are being forced to
  develop their own projects (monologue only); and that pre-decision
  development has been one of the biggest challenges historically (Faherty's
  own words are that early capital is more conservative because risk is
  greatest then).
- **The reactor trade-off.** Faherty himself concedes the AP1000 probably costs
  less per unit; his case for a smaller reactor over the large one rests on
  siting, since fewer sites suit the large one, most of them at what the
  transcript renders as "existing new nuclear locations" (the phrase looks
  garbled). Nobody pressed on the BWRX-300's maturity: he cites its lineage and
  the Darlington unit, which is itself, in his words, a first-of-a-kind project,
  and still under construction.
- **What the episode does not reach.** No project cost (he says only that
  Elementl has "an idea" of the overnight capital cost), no schedule, no named
  customer, and no discussion of the licensing process beyond coordinating the
  license with construction. Kann asks for lessons from Vogtle, V.C. Summer and
  projects abroad; the answer draws on Vogtle and Barakah only.
