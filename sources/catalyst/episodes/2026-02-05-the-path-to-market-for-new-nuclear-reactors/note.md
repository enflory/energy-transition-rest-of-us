## The question

With a wave of announcements from advanced nuclear companies, which milestones
actually signal progress toward a working reactor, and when will one reach the
grid?

## The answer

Huff ranks the announcements. Letters of intent, memoranda of understanding,
pre-application meetings (which produce no NRC decision) and "zero power"
criticality tests sit at the light end; NRC approvals, hot full power operation
and, for micro-reactors, a reactor factory are the real signals. She expects
construction permit decisions for the leading companies in late 2026 or 2027 and
would be surprised not to see new advanced reactor power on the grid by 2032.

## The argument

Licensing is long and iterative, and much of it produces no decision at all. A
company starts with a design, a site and pre-application engagement with the
Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC), then submits safety and site reports,
operations and training plans and a financial plan, through public meetings and
rounds of technical back-and-forth. Kann suggests this looks more collaborative
than "file and wait," and wonders whether productive design-review sessions mean
anything. Huff's answer is that engagement shows the company is active and the
regulator is informed, but the NRC issues no official decisions during
pre-application. The first real test is whether an application is accepted for
review, and rejections happen there, sometimes years in, often because the
application lacks technical depth. She also cites the NRC's denial of Oklo's
combined application, which sought both permits in parallel without a separate
design certification; it was rejected mainly for gaps on potential accidents and
on how safety systems were classified.

Nor is the slow step necessarily the one you would guess. Kann's intuition is
that design certification is hardest. Huff says the statistics vary by reactor,
so it is hard to say definitively, and points instead to environmental review,
which often runs in parallel, can be the longest step, and can rival the safety
review in length and review time regardless of design. The
regulator is also under pressure in two directions at once: it is being pushed
to move faster and has bipartisan legislation (which the transcript calls the
Advance Act) offering ways to accelerate, but it has fewer staff than before
the current administration, faces leadership vacancies, and is absorbing
executive orders meant to align it more closely with the White House.

The same skepticism applies to criticality. The Department of Energy's pilot
program challenges at least three companies to reach criticality by July 4,
2026, but a zero power test simply checks that neutrons can be multiplied, and
can be achieved without real engineering progress on fuel or design. What Huff
wants to see is hot full power criticality, and historically that has come a
median of about two years after concrete is poured. Most reactors are not on
pace, she says, because the deadline is extremely aggressive and perhaps unfair
to an industry building high-precision machines. Because the department did not
specify which kind of criticality it meant, companies may be able to declare
victory on the less meaningful version.

## What you need to know first

- **Part 50, Part 52, Part 53.** The NRC's three licensing pathways. Part 50
  separates the construction permit from the operating license, Part 52 combines
  them, and Part 53 is a newer route for advanced reactors that, per Huff, no
  one is really using yet.
- **Design certification.** A review that certifies the reactor design itself as
  safe independent of any site, so later applications only have to address
  site-specific questions.
- **Zero power versus hot full power criticality.** Criticality here means a
  reactor multiplying neutrons on its own. A zero power ("cold") test only
  checks that it can; a
  hot full power test runs fuel at real power densities and checks whether
  models match reality.

## Details worth keeping

- Huff's ladder of signals, weakest to strongest: a letter of intent; submitting
  a construction permit application or safety analysis report; NRC approval of a
  construction permit, design certification or operating license. On the utility
  side, a partnership on an early site permit application means far more than a
  memorandum of understanding.
- For micro-reactors she is looking for a reactor factory, since they do not
  make economic sense until factory-built. She names Holtec, a longtime maker of
  fuel casks, as having a fabrication advantage.
- Micro-reactors face the same licensing options as large reactors, and many
  advanced designs use Part 50 or 52 with waivers rather than Part 53. She
  credits the NRC with working through micro-reactor-specific issues, such as
  emergency planning zones that may not need to extend past the site fence.
- Valor Atomics reached criticality at Los Alamos, but Huff characterizes it as
  a cold zero power test that did not bring fuel to real power densities.
- Radiant's claim (as relayed by Kann) that its Kaleido reactor will be the
  first to achieve a "fueled test" in over 50 years by 2026 would be, per Huff,
  the hot full power kind.
- On waste, the NRC requires a plan before a reactor operates. With no final
  repository, companies plan interim on-site management (cooling pools, then dry
  cask storage) until the federal government takes ownership; some may add
  recycling.
- X-Energy and Kairos have had recent positive license announcements; Kairos
  already holds construction permits for test reactors. For TerraPower and
  others, the next milestone to watch is construction permit approval.

## Claims worth citing

All as stated on 2026-02-05. Regulatory timelines and company milestones in this
sector move quickly.

- The Department of Energy pilot program picked 11 early-stage companies.
  (Kann)
- A University of Illinois research and test micro-reactor is on a similar
  timeline to the TerraPower, X-Energy and Kairos permit decisions. The first
  grid reactor will likely be one reactor, followed shortly by others. (Huff)
- The legislation the transcript calls the Advance Act offers levers such as
  sharing international information and streamlining the use of former fossil
  fuel sites. The NRC also faces "numerous deadlines." (Huff)
- Executive orders in February and May of the year before the episode aimed to
  align independent regulatory commissions, including the NRC, with the White
  House, including possible changes to how dose rates are treated, with a
  rulemaking guidance deadline of February 23. (Huff)

## Where it's contested

- **The July 4 milestone.** Huff's skepticism (above) is about the deadline and
  its loose definition, not about any one company.
- **Kann's intuitions get qualified.** He wonders whether pre-application
  engagement signals progress and assumes design certification is the slowest
  step. Huff qualifies the first (it shows activity, but the NRC makes no
  decisions during pre-application), and on the second says it is hard to say
  definitively and points to environmental review instead.
- **The 2032 view carries a caveat.** Construction delays could erode investor
  confidence and slow the whole process. The monologue's line that most
  companies aim to commercialize within a couple of years or this decade is
  Kann's framing, not her forecast.
- **Her own institution is in the race.** She counts a University of Illinois
  micro-reactor among the near-term permit candidates.

  reasoning and hedging may not appear.
