## The question

What does the ocean, and defense in particular, demand of an energy system,
and how is autonomy changing those demands?

## The answer

Now that autonomy works well enough to be trusted, energy has become the
constraint on unmanned maritime vehicles. Nuss does not look to an exotic new
chemistry to fix that. He relies on commercial lithium-ion cells, repackaged
to survive the sea, and names two things that would change the game: getting
new commercial cells into the water faster (packaging, testing, certification)
and shared recharging depots at sea. Nuclear, for commercial shipping and as
low-power nuclear batteries, comes up as early and interesting rather than
imminent.

## The argument

Nuss starts from why water is hard. Salt water is harsh on robotic systems and
their energy systems, and pressure at depth crushes anything not built for it,
so an off-the-shelf lithium-ion battery dropped into an underwater vehicle will
not survive at operational depth. Propulsion is costly too, because water is
far denser than air (see Claims). A vehicle has one energy system serving both
propulsion and its sensors and payloads, and it must carry every bit of that
energy from the pier, because there is nowhere at sea to stop and refill.

Kann suggests that since propulsion dominates, adding autonomy changes little.
Nuss disagrees. For roughly two decades, he says, both energy and autonomy
limited vehicle design: robots rarely ran more than an hour or two and were
usually tethered to a crewed platform. Now that autonomy has been shown to be
trustworthy, vehicles are starting to range further, and energy is the weakness
that shows. What defense customers want, he says, is persistence and
flexibility: long-range access to an area, often without being detected, then
enough endurance to loiter there until called on to act, and then to get back
out. Nuss says users' sense of what is possible is shaped by nuclear
submarines, and part of his job is explaining that a small vehicle involves
compromises.

Why not simply use the densest fuel? Kann proposes that for vehicles too small
for a reactor, diesel probably makes the most sense in most cases, and adds
that diesel has maintenance problems of its own. Nuss's "yeah, for sure" follows
that point about the drawbacks, and he goes on to list more of them rather than
endorsing diesel: a diesel-electric vehicle has to surface regularly to run its
engine and recharge its batteries, spending energy on each ascent and descent,
contending with nets and trawlers, and giving off heat and a detectable
signature while it does. He says fuel cells have their own operational
problems. Buoyancy-engine gliders offer long endurance but little onboard
power, so they suit niche jobs. Lithium-ion's advantage is scale. Undersea
vehicles are a small fraction of the cell market, so the sector will always be
a fast follower of commercial and EV technology.

The catch is the time between a commercial cell existing and that cell running
certified in a vehicle at sea. Cells have to go into a pressure vessel or be
potted into a pressure-tolerant block, which takes specialized, capital-heavy
equipment. Then the pack has to pass a certification process before it can
operate from a given ship or base. Nuss calls lost access to new technology a
bigger cost than the testing itself: a system that takes years to certify has
missed several rounds of commercial improvement. So one of his two wishes is to
shorten that timeline. The other is a network of open recharging depots on the
surface and on the seabed, which would let vehicles carry much less energy and
be built smaller.

## What you need to know first

- **Pressure vessel versus pressure-tolerant packaging.** The two ways to
  protect battery cells at depth: seal them inside a dry housing that resists
  the ocean's pressure, or encase the stacked cells in epoxy or a similar
  compound so the whole block can withstand the pressure directly.
- **Persistence.** How long a vehicle can stay present and useful once it
  reaches an area. Nuss treats it as separate from long-range access, the
  ability to get there in the first place.
- **Diesel-electric.** A setup where a diesel engine recharges batteries that
  drive the vehicle. Underwater, this means coming back to the surface to run
  the engine.
- **Buoyancy engine.** A glider moves oil between bladders to change its
  density and uses small wings to turn sinking and rising into forward motion.
  It has long range but little power to spare.

## Details worth keeping

- Nuss says submarine nuclear power is not only about on-station time but
  about the energy and flexibility to travel far, stay a long time, and get
  back out.
- Anduril buys no cells from what Nuss calls adversary countries, and he says
  tracing the supply chain many layers down is complicated. The company tries
  not to depend on a specialized battery industry. Instead it aims to send
  demand signals through its battery partners to their cell suppliers. He hopes
  the industry might take some risk, maybe investing ahead of need, and perhaps
  jump-start a new cell formulation.
- Reliability is not uniform. Sensitive or high-value missions need a battery
  that brings the vehicle home. For a cheap, expendable system, Nuss says a
  far less reliable battery might be acceptable.
- Some safety rules exist for good reason, especially for submarines. Nuss
  argues that commercial EV regulations should be adoptable for defense uses,
  while defense adds certification requirements of its own on top.
- Asked about surface vessels, Nuss describes commercial shipping as stagnant
  and organized around fuel prices, with some renewed talk of wind assist. He
  says nuclear has become a more popular topic in the last year or two, and
  notes the US Maritime Administration has issued public requests for
  information on nuclear-powered commercial shipping. He points to large
  policy, regulatory and investment hurdles.
- Nuclear batteries and radiovoltaic devices, borrowed from space
  applications, are low-power. He sees uses in persistent sensing, maybe not in
  vehicles. The barriers are isotope and component supply chains and a
  regulatory path he says has not been exercised in maritime in a long time.

## Claims worth citing

All figures as stated on 2026-09-24 and attributed to the speaker. Market size
and certification times are rough and offered loosely.

- Water is about 1,000x denser than air, so moving a vehicle one nautical mile
  takes about 1,000x more energy than in air. Nuss derives the energy figure
  from the density ratio. (Nuss)
- Nuclear submarines can provide around 90 days on station. (Nuss)
- Diesel-electric underwater vehicles must surface to recharge about every
  couple of days. (Nuss)
- Battery certification for defense use "can take up to a year or two." In the
  same answer he poses a case where it takes 2 to 3 years, so the range is
  loose. (Nuss)
- Shared depots might let vehicles carry about 25% of the energy they carry
  now. Nuss floats one every 150 miles as possibly interesting. (Nuss)
- Underwater vehicles typically operate at 2-3 knots, so slow recharging would
  be acceptable. (Nuss)
- The whole undersea-vehicle battery market is probably a couple hundred
  megawatt-hours, "off the cuff" and "if I had to guess." The industry is
  trying to forecast whether it reaches gigawatt-hours within three years,
  because of the demand signal it is seeing; he does not predict that it will.
  (Nuss)
- Commercial ships typically run at 10-12 knots, tuned to fuel price,
  consumption and emissions. Nuclear might allow 20-25 knots, and delivery in
  5 days instead of 7. (Nuss, citing white papers and conversations)
- Perhaps 10-20% of the commercial shipping market might eventually go
  nuclear, taking a couple of years to get there. (Nuss)
- A battery about 80% reliable might be acceptable for a low-cost, expendable
  system. (Nuss)

## Where it's contested

- **Kann's premises draw thin assent.** Kann proposes that diesel makes the
  most sense below reactor scale; Nuss never endorses that and answers with
  diesel's operational problems. When Kann says Anduril's side of the market is
  low volume with high willingness to pay, Nuss says "yeah" and talks only
  about reliability. Neither view should be read as Nuss's own.
- **Nuclear shipping economics go unanswered.** Kann asks how nuclear
  propulsion could make economic sense given what he presumes is a very high
  cost of energy. Nuss explicitly says he is not a commercial shipping expert
  and answers with operational and revenue possibilities (speed, premium
  delivery, new ports) rather than the cost of energy. That question is left
  open.
- **The rough figures in Claims are guesses**, as marked there, and the depot
  network is a wish: Nuss says its commercial viability has not been
  established.
- **Unexamined:** Nuss speaks from inside a company that sells into this
  market, and nobody tests the premise that energy, rather than cost, sensors
  or doctrine, is now the binding constraint.
