## The question

If data centers are going to build tens of gigawatts of onsite gas generation
anyway, how much methane will that release, and how much of it can be avoided?

## The answer

By Irzak's estimate, counting the methane that leaks or slips unburned roughly
doubles the warming impact people attribute to these plants on a 100-year
basis, and makes methane the majority of it on a 20-year basis. She puts 40 to
50% of the total as preventable with measures that are mostly cheap and, in
several cases, available now, and which she does not think would add more than
about 1% to a data center's cost or delay it. The other half has no easy answer.

## The argument

Kann opens by taking the buildout as a given: grid interconnection takes years,
so onsite gas is often the fastest route to firm power, at least today, and
the question is how it
gets built rather than whether. The usual emissions story counts the carbon
dioxide from burning the gas. Irzak's point is that methane escapes unburned at
four places along the chain: at the wellhead, in the gathering pipes that carry
gas to processing within the basin, through leaks at the data center site, and
as "slip" out of the exhaust of reciprocating engines. Long-distance
transmission pipelines, by contrast, leak a negligible amount. Because methane
is far more potent than carbon dioxide over short horizons, these losses carry
weight out of proportion to their volume.

Each stage has a wide spread, and for a nameable reason. Upstream leakage
varies enormously, both between basins and between operators within a basin, so
where a buyer sources gas matters. Slip is already significant at full load and
much higher at partial load, which is exactly how some operators run engines
when trying to follow solar output. Facility leaks are the least measured, but
Irzak argues they should be higher than at a utility plant: where a utility
might meet a gigawatt with two turbines and a second stage in one building,
some data centers are using around 35 reciprocating engines spread over a large
area, multiplying the pipes, welds and connections that can leak.

Put together for 50 gigawatts of new behind-the-meter capacity, under what she
calls optimistic facility-leak assumptions, about 380 megatons of carbon
dioxide equivalent results, of which roughly 40 to 50% is methane. That is the
basis for the doubling: anyone who counted only combustion has about half the
picture on a 100-year view, and on a 20-year view it is much worse, with
methane the majority. She calls the preventable portion the biggest climate
lever she has had in her career.

The case for prevention rests on cost and speed. Buyers can choose lower-leak
basins or certified low-leak producers within a basin, whose premium she calls
pretty low; they can write leak requirements and certification into
construction contracts and demand leak detection with fast repair; and they can
avoid partial-load operation and add exhaust catalysts or oxidizers. She
allows that buyers may not do all this immediately because of the race, and
Kann pushes back: if, as she says, none of it slows a project, what is the
excuse? Irzak concedes the point but raises scale questions she cannot answer:
certified low-leak supply might run short, catalysts might too, and slip
catalysts are not yet a solved problem. The remaining half has, in her words,
only two direct remedies: onsite capture, which would take about 15% of the
plant's energy and which she thinks will be hard to sell, because operators
will not give up energy that is not going to compute; and offsets. She also
mentions pairing with more renewables or the grid (which brings back the
interconnection wait), using the gas less over time, and leaving room for a
second stage.

## What you need to know first

- **Carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e).** A way of adding methane and carbon
  dioxide on one scale by weighting methane by its warming potency. The weight
  depends on the time horizon chosen, which is why the totals differ so much
  between the 100-year and 20-year views.
- **Behind-the-meter gas.** Generation built on the data center's own site
  rather than drawing from the grid.
- **Methane slip.** Methane that passes through an engine unburned and leaves
  in the exhaust. Irzak says it is a significant issue for reciprocating
  engines and not for turbines, combined-cycle plants or fuel cells.
- **Second stage.** An addition to a gas plant that, Irzak says, gets 50% more
  energy from exactly the same gas. She does not describe how it works.

## Details worth keeping

- Wellhead leakage varies somewhat with gas prices ("a little bit of that
  correlation"), per a study she cites from RMI (not spelled out on air): when
  prices are high, producers spend more on inspections; when low, it is not
  worth paying someone to find leaks. She notes a big recent improvement in New
  Mexico and suggests prices are now the main driver given that EPA rules
  "potentially" did not come in.
- High-leak basins she names: the Permian, the Uinta, and "San Joaquin in New
  Mexico" (as rendered). Gas from Pennsylvania's Marcellus leaks much less at
  both wellhead and gathering pipes.
- She argues data center buyers, especially if they formed a coalition for
  responsible gas buying, could decide "who drills the next hole" in the Permian
  and tip the whole industry to the low-leak regime. BP is one of the certified
  low-leak producers in the Permian, so low-leak supply is not niche.
- Fast leak repair onsite matters for the bottom line as well as the climate,
  because by that point the operator has already paid for the gas; it is also a
  safety issue with pressurized, flammable gas.
- Hyperscaler emissions accounting mostly relies on averages and, she says,
  under-counts methane in a lot of places; satellite observations do not match.
  She does not fault the accountants, since almost no one in the data center
  chain knows what methane slip is.
- She would not say which hyperscalers are ahead, citing NDAs, but says the
  difference between them is large and that internal organization shapes who
  can move how fast.
- What she calls one of the biggest mistakes: builders are laying out sites
  that cannot accept a second stage later.
- Separately, she says builders racing to deploy are not leaving "hooks" to add
  catalysts later, and that a third party like her firm is useful for pointing
  these things out.
- She expects spillover: if data centers move the gas industry, cheaper leak
  detection and bolt-on slip catalysts could become standard for any
  reciprocating engine, as catalytic converters did for cars.

## Claims worth citing

All figures as stated on 2026-10-22 and attributed to Irzak unless noted;
the emissions totals are her firm's modeling, not measurements.

- Methane is 28 times more potent than carbon dioxide over 100 years and 84
  times over 20 years, and accounts for 30% of current warming. (Irzak)
- Methane's air-quality effect: 850,000 premature deaths a year and "860
  million crop loss," with no unit given. (Irzak)
- About 4% of Permian production is lost across wellhead and gathering pipes
  combined, on average. On the upstream side alone, the best players are
  "probably" leaking about 0.2% and the worst at least double the average.
  (Irzak)
- Four-stroke lean-burn engines slip about 3% of incoming methane at full load
  (EPA average, cited by Irzak); she has seen measurements up to 12% at partial
  load. (Irzak)
- Her model assumes 50 gigawatts of new behind-the-meter gas; latest reports
  suggest more like 75. For scale, California has 40 gigawatts of gas
  generation installed. (Irzak)
- Total methane reaching the atmosphere assumed at 7.5%, which she describes as
  using optimistic facility-leak assumptions. (Irzak)
- About 380 megatons of carbon dioxide equivalent added to US emissions "in the
  next 18 months for those 50 gigawatts to come online," 50 to 60% from
  combustion and 40 to 50% methane (100-year basis). The phrasing leaves it
  unclear whether that is a cumulative 18-month figure or an annual rate once
  built. (Irzak)
- On a 20-year basis the same total is about 724 megatons, the majority methane.
  (Irzak)
- The preventable share, "call it 50%," equals 15% of US grid emissions; the
  base period for that comparison is not stated. (Irzak)

## Where it's contested

- **The host's monologue goes further than the guest does on air.** Kann says
  that by Irzak's math an onsite gas plant looks "kind of like a coal plant,"
  that the aggregate could resemble all of global aviation, and that choices
  produce roughly a 2x swing. Irzak never makes the coal or aviation comparison
  in the conversation; the figures she gives are the ones above.
- **Data quality is uneven, by her own account.** Slip data at full load is
  solid; basin data is good but sometimes a couple of years old; facility leaks
  are the weakest, estimated rather than measured, with satellite views that
  look "not so great for some players."
- **Whether there is any excuse.** Kann argues that if mitigation does not slow
  projects, the race cannot justify putting it off. Irzak agrees in principle
  but raises possible shortages of certified gas and catalysts, and says she
  does not know how much influence she has.
- **Aeroderivative turbines.** Asked whether they slip methane, she thinks not
  but says she would need to double-check.
- **The guest's stake.** Irzak describes her company as a third party "like us"
  that talks builders through these choices, and proposes a central body setting
  leak standards the way LEED does for buildings ("we set the leak standards").
  The estimates, the cost claim and the proposed remedy come from the same
  source and nobody independent tests them in the episode.
