## The question

Texas built a huge fleet of grid batteries and the money dried up. Will data
center load growth bring the profits back, and when?

## The answer

Vermillion expects a recovery but not soon. Modo's own model shows price
volatility returning in a big way around 2029 to 2030, enough to clear the
roughly $100 per kilowatt that a new battery needs for at least a couple of
years. It could come sooner if load arrives fast and coincides with extreme
weather, and he stresses that nobody has enough information on demand growth
to know the timing. Until then, today's fleet earns well below what it needs.

## The argument

The collapse is mostly self-inflicted. Texas batteries first made their money
from ancillary services: payments for standing ready to steady the grid's
frequency or cover a sudden loss of supply. In 2022 and 2023 those services
supplied most of a battery's revenue. But the grid operator only buys so much
of them, and once installed battery capacity outgrew that amount, batteries
moved into energy arbitrage, charging when power is cheap and selling when it
is expensive. That market is far deeper, since Texas serves 60 to 80
gigawatts of load at peak, but the same mechanism followed them there. The
more batteries compete to be dispatched in the high-price hours, the lower
they have to offer, and the more they suppress the volatility they earn from.
Vermillion calls this cannibalization. Revenue fell from
nearly $200 per installed kilowatt in 2023, a hot summer when ancillary
services were not yet saturated, to around $30 over the last 12 months.
That is well below what a new two-hour system needs to be financed.

Kann raises the obvious confounder: Texas price spikes come from weather, and
mild weather alone suppresses them. Vermillion grants weather a real role but
places it second. The majority of the decline comes from how much battery
capacity was built, with weather as a compounding layer on top. He is careful
that recent summers were not cool, just not extreme. Extreme weather can still
produce spikes, and his account of Storm Fern in January shows that the
fleet's own behavior now shapes them. When forecasts missed and overnight
real-time prices stayed high, many operators hesitated to recharge, so the
next morning only about a third of the fleet's capacity was charged and prices
reached four figures. How much charge the fleet is holding has become a key
variable in
its own right.

Against that sits the case for building anyway, which Vermillion lays out as
the developer's thesis. Power markets are cyclical, infrastructure responds
with a lag, and low prices cure low prices. What is unusual this time is the
scale and speed of the demand that wants to connect, mainly data centers. The
headline requests are very obviously inflated, but even a tenth of them would be
transformational for a grid that peaks at 91 gigawatts. Meanwhile he sees the
first signs of supply slowing, at least for the next year or two: the battery
queue has stopped growing, new installations are no
longer doubling each year, projects are withdrawing even after signing
interconnection agreements, and a merchant gas plant would face the same weak
prices a battery does. So demand could quickly outrun what is today an
oversupplied system. The catch is the gap. Even a developer convinced that
volatility returns in 2028 has to survive the lean years first, and a
third-party lender wants a contract that covers debt service in the meantime,
such as a toll, a revenue share or floor, or a swap. Buyers and sellers of
those contracts are far apart on price and length.

Kann pushes on the strongest objection to the recovery story. If new data
centers bring their own on-site generation, they could run it exactly when
prices spike, adding load without adding to the peak that batteries are
waiting for. Vermillion takes it seriously and builds it into the model, but
argues the timing works against it in the near term. Data centers arriving in
the next one to three years may already have secured grid connections because
the system is oversupplied, so they are probably going to lean less on their
own generation. Later, as the system
tightens, more will be offered partial connections and will bring their own
power. His rough rule of thumb, which he describes as substantially
guesswork, is that 50% to 70% of nameplate data center load ends up drawing
from the grid in the long run.

## What you need to know first

- **ERCOT.** The episode uses the name for both Texas's grid operator and the
  electricity market it runs, without spelling out the letters. Kann calls it
  "more or less" an energy-only market: there are no capacity payments to lean
  on, so resources earn mainly from the electricity they sell.
- **Merchant.** A project that sells into the market at whatever prices it
  finds, with no long-term contract fixing its revenue.
- **Ancillary services versus energy arbitrage.** The first is paid
  standby work keeping the grid stable, a lucrative but shallow market. The
  second is buying low and selling high on power prices, a deeper market whose
  returns depend on how volatile prices are.
- **Duration.** How many hours a battery can discharge at full power. Longer
  batteries can ride out a price spike that outlasts the first hour.

## Details worth keeping

- Duration in Texas has been lengthening. Batteries were almost all one-hour
  at first because ancillary services set no real duration rules. New rules on
  how much energy must back each megawatt of service, cheaper cells and the
  shift to arbitrage pushed the average toward two hours, with some 2.5 to 3
  hour and a couple of 4-hour projects now arriving. California's batteries
  are mostly four hours or more because of how its capacity rules are built.
- Some of this summer's high-price days came in two waves. Batteries
  discharged into the forecast peak hour, ran low, and then a second real-time
  spike an hour or two later, driven by a combination of things such as the
  wind and load forecasts, rewarded batteries that held charge back or were
  simply longer.
- Flatter round-the-clock loads are making the evening handoff off 30 to 40
  gigawatts of solar harder for the grid operator, which contributes to these
  spikes.
- Mergers and asset sales are happening, but less than the economics would
  suggest. Older projects earned solid returns from early ancillary services,
  or at least passable 2024 revenue that kept them above debt service, and many
  owners are large, well-capitalized power companies or utilities that may be
  able to weather the storm, perhaps having self-financed.
- Tolling deals have been a useful tool but are not the predominant form of
  contract. Any such hedge will scale down the developer's upside quite a bit,
  especially at a relatively low strike price; that is the trade-off accepted
  for financing.
- Vermillion expects the same arc in other markets. Batteries always go to
  ancillary services first, saturate them, and move on. PJM, another US grid
  market that the episode does not further describe, redesigned its
  regulation market last year and prices have been extremely high, with
  paybacks in a single year, but that market is shallow and should compress
  once batteries there reach one to two gigawatts.
  For a low-growth market like California, he asks whether recovery would
  instead come from thermal plant retirements changing the price shape.
- Kann adds that ancillary-service saturation is a recurring story in
  batteries, citing PJM as where the first grid-scale batteries went.

## Claims worth citing

All figures as stated on 2026-10-01. Fleet size, queue and revenue figures in
this market move quickly.

- Texas had about 200 megawatts of grid-scale batteries at the start of the
  decade and 16.5 gigawatts, nearly 29 gigawatt-hours, by the end of June
  2026. (Kann)
- Vermillion gives the fleet several ways within the episode: 17 to 18
  gigawatts fully approved for commercial operation and over 20 gigawatts
  having completed construction early on, then 18 to 19 gigawatts commercially
  operational "today" from a start-of-year base of about 14 gigawatts, with 5
  to 6 gigawatts of additions expected by year end. The figures do not fully
  reconcile, so check the transcript before quoting one. (Vermillion)
- About 5 to 6 gigawatts were added in 2025. (Vermillion; Kann puts it at 6
  gigawatts)
- The average Texas battery earned roughly 84% less in 2025 than in 2023. In
  2023 there were 58 days on which it earned at least 50 cents per kilowatt;
  in 2025 there were three. (Kann)
- A two-hour battery in Texas needs roughly $100 per installed kilowatt, maybe
  a little less, to be financeable. He gives it as the rule of thumb Modo has
  heard and calls it a shifting target as costs move. (Vermillion)
- In 2022 to 2023 most batteries earned more than 90% of their revenue from
  ancillary services (later in the same answer, 80% to 90% in 2023); today
  close to 80% comes from energy arbitrage. (Vermillion)
- The battery interconnection queue has stalled at 150 to 160 gigawatts after
  growing by dozens of gigawatts a year. (Vermillion)
- Something like 13 to 15 gigawatts of project capacity withdrew after
  signing interconnection agreements, once a near guarantee of being built; he
  introduces it as something Modo "tracked last year," which leaves unclear
  whether the withdrawals or the tracking fell last year. (Vermillion) Kann's
  monologue gives a record 13.7 gigawatts withdrawn in the first half of this
  year and says new queue entries have halved from their peak. The two may be
  the same figure stated loosely; they are not reconciled on air.
- Projects wanting to connect, primarily data centers, total 400 to 500
  gigawatts (rendered "4 or 500"), a figure he calls very obviously inflated.
  He expects connection to stretch over five to six years. (Vermillion)
- Overnight before the critical morning of the January storm, real-time prices
  ran around $150 against $50 day-ahead. (Vermillion)
- More than 20 gigawatts of aging coal and gas in Texas is losing money, and
  some will shut. (Kann)
- PJM's regulation market is about 600 to 700 megawatts deep, against about
  500 megawatts of batteries there today. (Vermillion)

## Where it's contested

- **The timing is explicitly uncertain.** Vermillion says there is not enough
  information on demand growth to know how fast it arrives, and that his model
  date could be pulled earlier by fast connections combined with a severe
  summer or winter. Both speakers mention ERCOT's "Batch Zero" process, which
  the episode does not explain; Kann says it may delay some data centers, and
  Vermillion ties faster demand growth to its being finalized next year. Kann
  says outright he has no idea when recovery comes.
- **The monologue's macro case is Kann's.** The money-losing coal and gas,
  sold-out gas turbines and batteries as the resource that can show up on time
  are stated by Kann; Vermillion's case rests on load growth outpacing slowing
  supply.
- **"Collapsed" is hedged.** Vermillion calls it a strong word before
  conceding that is roughly what happened to revenue.
- **Weather versus overbuild.** Kann frames the weather question as hard to
  untangle; Vermillion assigns the majority to overbuild without putting a
  number on the split.
- **On-site generation could blunt the recovery.** Raised by Kann as "one
  maybe big risk." Vermillion's answer depends on a near-term versus
  long-term timing
  argument and a grid-draw ratio he describes as largely guesstimated.
- **What went unexamined.** The $100 hurdle rests on costs Vermillion calls a
  shifting target. His model accounts for the queue, but the episode does not
  quantify whether batteries still being added this year push recovery past
  the model's date, a tension Kann names in the monologue.
