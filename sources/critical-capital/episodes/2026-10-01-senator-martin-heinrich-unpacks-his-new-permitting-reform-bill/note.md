## The question

What does the new bipartisan permitting bill actually change about how energy
infrastructure gets approved and paid for, and can it pass?

## The answer

Heinrich's answer is that the bill keeps environmental and historic review
standards where they are but forces decisions, yes or no, to arrive much
faster, and that from his committee's point of view it is largely a
transmission bill. It also makes it harder for an administration to favor one
kind of energy over another, and makes large new loads such as data centers pay
for the grid upgrades they cause. Its passage is not settled: a piece of the
overall deal is still being negotiated with the White House, and the Senate has
only a few legislative days left in the year.

## The argument

Heinrich's starting point is that delay itself is the cost. He watched the
SunZia transmission line take 17 years from start to energization, long enough
that the first developer gave up and another bought the project, and he
presents that as proof of how hard big things are to build even when the
project is excellent. Hence the bill's organizing idea, which he states in
nearly the same words twice: the same standards, but get to yes, or to no for a
bad project, a whole lot faster, because time is money in infrastructure. The
mechanisms are procedural. Reviews under the National Environmental Policy Act
and the National Historic Preservation Act run in parallel rather than in
sequence, and deadlines he calls shot clocks cap environmental reviews. He
frames this as speed, not deregulation, and points to a tightening in one
place: some tribal consultation under the historic preservation law, which
until now rested on an executive order, is written into statute.

Transmission is where much of his committee's drafting time went, and his
reasoning turns on where the bottleneck now sits. Wind and solar have grown
fast for a decade, and on his account the constraint has moved from building
generation to moving it. The bill adds medium- and long-term planning, lets
existing lines be reconductored without a complete new environmental impact
statement, and strengthens the federal backstop that lets the federal energy
regulator site a line when a state does not act. The detail he singles out is
that the regulator can study a route while it waits for the state, so that if
the backstop is needed, the year already spent is not lost. But new lines take
years however fast they are permitted, so he also reaches for what can help
sooner. He says the meantime should require technologies that get more out of
existing wires (without making clear whether that requirement is in this
bill), and the bill itself opens the bulk power market to virtual power plants.
On money, the host notes that Heinrich recently put out separate legislation
for a transmission investment tax credit. Heinrich argues such a credit would do
more to get clean generation built than even some generation tax credits,
because financial certainty draws more and faster capital to long linear
projects.

Two provisions answer political problems rather than engineering ones. Against
an administration he describes as slow-walking wind and solar, the bill lets a
sector that can show from permitting data that it was singled out take an
expedited path to court, with fines that can be levied on the offending agency.
That provision sits beside an unfinished negotiation over returning to regular
permitting, which he says is needed to land the overall deal. On data centers,
he contrasts what he calls a messaging bill, which failed in the Senate that
day, with a mandate in this one: incremental generation, transmission or
distribution costs caused by a large new load are charged to its developer and
cannot be passed to other customers in the same utility's territory. His
reasoning is that voluntary commitments have already failed, since rate hikes
have reached consumers in some markets.

## What you need to know first

- **Permitting reform.** Changes to the federal approval process that energy
  projects must clear, here mainly environmental review, historic review and
  transmission siting, rather than changes to what is allowed to be built.
- **Shot clock.** A statutory deadline by which an agency must finish a review.
  The aim is to end open-ended analysis, not to change its standard.
- **Federal backstop siting authority.** The power of the federal energy
  regulator, which Heinrich calls FERC without spelling it out, to approve a
  transmission route when a state has not acted on it.
- **Virtual power plant.** Many small devices, such as rooftop solar, electric
  vehicles and smart thermostats, coordinated so that each gives up a little on
  a peak day and together they supply capacity like a power plant.

## Details worth keeping

- The episode was recorded immediately after the press conference announcing
  the bill, which the host's monologue dates to September 30. Claims about what
  happened "today" refer to that day.
- Heinrich wore his father's 60-years-of-service pin from the International
  Brotherhood of Electrical Workers, representing a career in the utility and
  power sector. The host says his father was a lineman and that he believes a
  SunZia substation is named for him.
- Heinrich describes SunZia as having the largest project labor agreement in
  Southwestern history and, at the time, the largest clean generation project in
  the Western Hemisphere.
- The bill tries to put geothermal drilling on Bureau of Land Management land on
  an equal footing with oil drilling. Heinrich says new methods of extracting
  heat, which no longer need the super-hot sources found in a handful of places,
  mean half the West could potentially produce clean power. Separately, startups
  are using AI on old and newly gathered data to find resources that were
  missed. He calls geothermal baseload and, in many cases depending on storage,
  dispatchable.
- On data centers his personal approach, stated as his preference rather than as
  bill text, is high standards instead of a moratorium: low water use, union
  jobs, clean power, then local communities decide.
- Beyond this bill he points to converting power into fuels and chemicals for
  industry and transport, and industrial heat pumps, as the next work, and notes
  three fusion startups in Albuquerque and a small modular reactor startup in
  New Mexico.

## Claims worth citing

All figures as stated in an episode recorded on the day the bill was announced
and published 2026-10-01. The bill's contents are as described by one of its
negotiators and may change before any vote.

- Federal permitting delays clean energy projects by six months on average, and
  cancellations due to litigation are quite common. This is from a Crux study,
  cited by the host in his monologue; Heinrich never discusses it. (Johnson)
- Shot clocks: two years for an environmental impact statement, one year for an
  environmental assessment. Today such reviews can stretch to as much as a
  decade. (Heinrich)
- SunZia took 17 years, close to 18, from first starting to energization a few
  months before recording. (Heinrich) The host puts it at 16 or 17. (Johnson)
- The biggest transmission bill to date, in Heinrich's description from his
  committee's point of view. (Heinrich)
- Aggregated virtual power plants could bring gigawatts of capacity to the grid
  on a hard summer day. No more precise figure is given. (Heinrich)
- Solar panels went from something like $77 per watt to well below a dollar.
  (Heinrich)
- It should not be 100 times harder to drill a geothermal well than an oil well
  on federal land; the figure is his illustration of the disparity the bill
  removes. (Heinrich)
- If its costs can be brought down to be competitive, geothermal could help
  decarbonize the hardest part of the grid, the last 20% or so. (Heinrich)
- Next steps: the Senate returns November 9, after the midterms (Johnson), and
  Heinrich hopes "Leader Thune" brings it to the floor as the Senate's next
  business on the Monday after the election. (Heinrich)

## Where it's contested

Nobody contests anything. The host affirms throughout and the guest is one of
the bill's negotiators, so what matters here is what the episode leaves open.

- **The deal is not finished.** Heinrich says agreement with the White House on
  returning to regular permitting has not been landed and is necessary for the
  overall deal, and calls the talks constructive. The host says members of
  Heinrich's own caucus were questioning the administration's commitment that
  morning.
- **The host's framing runs ahead of the guest.** The monologue says permitting
  reform can unlock an extraordinary amount of clean energy, and the host's
  introduction says this bill could revolutionize infrastructure. Heinrich makes
  the narrower claim that the bill's net effect will be to help hold down
  electricity costs, create jobs and protect resources. Neither puts a number on
  any of it, and the one figure offered for the cost of delay is the host's
  company's, not the guest's.
- **The guest has a stake as negotiator.** He is a lead Democratic negotiator
  describing his own bill on the day it was released, and his account of what
  it does is not tested against the text or against any critic.
- **Not stated on air.** The host's question describes the failed ratepayer
  measure as Republican-led and says Heinrich voted against it; Heinrich
  discusses why it failed but does not confirm his own vote. How the large-load
  cost mandate would be enforced is not discussed.
- **The bill's name is ambiguous in the transcript.** Only the host names it,
  twice, as "bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act" with a lower-case
  first word. Heinrich never names it, so whether "Bipartisan" is part of the
  formal title is unclear from the conversation.
