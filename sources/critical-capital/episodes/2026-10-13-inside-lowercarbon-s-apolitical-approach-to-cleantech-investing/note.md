## The question

If clean technology now competes on price, can it be funded and sold as
ordinary business, without climate framing or government help, and what is
actually standing in the way of building it at scale?

## The answer

Sacca and Kelley say yes to the first part: their companies mostly make
substitutes for things that already have a market, at parity or better on
price, so they can be sold on cost, jobs and supply security and modelled like
any other business. What stands in the way, on their account, is mostly not
the technology. Sacca names litigious environmental groups as the main
opposition, and both guests describe finance that lags the economics: Sacca
blames lenders and corporate buyers who demand step-by-step demonstrations, and
Kelley describes a capital stack founders must assemble from many kinds of
money for risk-averse later-stage underwriters. Sacca's policy stance is to
build businesses that need nobody's permission.

## The argument

Sacca's starting point is a change in the economics. At Lowercarbon's founding,
he says, addressing climate change had always needed either philanthropy or
some regulatory mandate; now the core products have reached price parity, or
better, with what they replace. Fossil fuel, he says, is expensive to dig up and
move, sometimes takes wars to secure, and occasionally blows up. So decarbonizing
a process is good for the environment but, more importantly for the firm's work,
a chance to profit; and because that work is more bipartisan without the word,
the firm's internal rule is to stop saying "climate." His evidence is a bank that refused to invest in a
climate fund and then said yes to the same fund described as energy, advanced
manufacturing and resilience. He cites polling data, without naming its source,
ranking climate and clean energy 23rd and 24th of 25 voter issues, while the
economy, living costs and energy costs lead. The pitch he would make as a
candidate is lower bills, domestic jobs and less dependence on autocratic
regimes, which he says is exactly what the companies do.

The second step is why these businesses are easier to judge than the software
companies Sacca backed before. Twitter or Uber created new behavior with no
demand curve to model. Almost everything Lowercarbon funds is a substitute good:
if you make clean steel, you already know the size of the market, what buyers
pay, and the specifications they need, so the question reduces to whether you
can get the cost down far enough that even an oil company would buy it because
it is the right steel. Kelley makes the financing version of the point: once a
company has retired most of its science risk, it is a very financeable asset
with purchase agreements and visible unit economics. Founders need to learn to
tell what the firm calls the "add water" story: a dollar of capital in produces,
in her example, ten times the revenue out. Sacca stresses
that the work is now less creative and more analytical, and that the firm trades
on an arbitrage: much of the market still sees the sector as uninvestable, so
companies branded as climate companies are priced low.

The turn is that knowable economics have not produced fast money. Kelley says
no single form of capital is the answer: the next round will mix equity with
debt, and the debt itself comes in several sleeves, such as tax credits,
project finance, and a development capital facility above a project facility.
Sacca says the bigger problem is speed. Founders asked what they would do with
another billion dollars always have an immediate answer, yet a company he says
knows its technology works builds two levels of demonstration plant because its
banks require it, and hyperscaler staff want a pilot before signing for the
real thing. He attributes this to career protection, not doubt about the
technology. His own claim is that factory finance is not hard, since major
lenders already have spreadsheets for factory economics. Johnson replies that
it is still broken: the
companies are young, without credit ratings or a track record of building large
plants, and have to raise tens of billions in project capital from many parties.
Sacca accepts there is a gap and points to new lenders starting to fill it.

Sacca's counterexample is a single decision-maker accountable for the whole
system. Sacca describes the State of New Mexico, which funds social
programs from gas royalties and invests in electrifying its economy because it
knows gas wells decline unpredictably. Because the state sees jobs, lower
energy prices and opportunities for its graduates together, it decides quickly
and even finds sites. On policy, Sacca says he is "pretty convinced" the country
is in a post-policy phase and refuses to back any business that depends on
anyone's permission. He is not against subsidy: he took the Inflation Reduction
Act's money gladly and welcomes trade restrictions that help his companies. He
will not let a business depend on them. Kelley adds that US manufacturing must
be built for American conditions, fewer electricians but more software and AI,
rather than copying China.

## What you need to know first

- **Capital stack.** The layers of money a company combines to fund itself:
equity and several "sleeves" of debt, which Kelley lists as tax credits,
project finance, and a development capital facility sitting above a project
facility. Her point is that a growth-stage hardware company needs several at
once.
- **Substitute good.** A product that replaces one already sold, such as
clean-process steel or chemicals. Its demand, price and specifications are
known in advance, which is the basis of Sacca's claim that these companies are
easier to model than consumer software.
- **Science risk versus scale risk.** Whether the technology works at all,
versus whether it can be built and sold in volume. The guests' whole financing
argument assumes the first is retired before growth money arrives.
- **Scope 3 emissions.** Emissions in a company's supply chain, which Sacca
mentions only to say customers do not care or ask about them.

## Details worth keeping

- Sacca says the obstacles he meets most are not protesters but well-funded
environmental groups that sue at every step using environmental-review laws,
naming NEPA and CEQA, and calls them "the obstructionists."
- Kelley says AI demand has been an enormous accelerant for power companies,
but that an internal Lowercarbon view holds air conditioning to be a much
bigger driver of electricity demand than AI or data centers. Sacca lists AI, a
growing global middle class and the need for cooling as demand drivers.
- Kelley says talent coming out of OpenAI, Anthropic, SpaceX and Anduril, much
of it freed by large exits, wants purposeful work, which gives the firm
founders to back and draws talent to its companies.
- Solugen, which makes industrial chemicals with enzymes instead of oil, has
customers who ask three questions: is it the same chemical, is it cheaper, and
can it ship from down the road. Emissions never come up. Its teams test with
plumbing parts from Home Depot.
- Sacca says early teams can now rent cloud compute for tens of thousands of
dollars and share bioreactor space, where a physical plant might once have
cost a quarter billion. He compares this to cheap web tools around 2005 freeing
software founders from big upfront raises.
- Lowercarbon runs what Sacca calls the world's only dedicated fusion fund and
now a second one, backed after partner Clay Dumas saw machine learning speed up
fusion experiments. The surname appears in square brackets in the transcript.
- Sacca names Arbor, which 3D-prints small turbines using what he calls a
supercritical process. He says it runs cheaper than natural gas with clean
water as a byproduct, but faces a demonstration-plant roadmap he finds too
slow.
- Sacca says what he calls "Palmer Luckey's bank" is now lending to some
portfolio companies; Kelley calls Anduril an enormous partner at the 10 to 100
unit scale.

## Where it's contested

- **Whether finance is broken or merely slow.** The monologue calls the project
finance stack broken and lenders unable to underwrite new technology. Sacca
says factory finance problems "aren't hard" and blames risk-averse
institutional habits. Kelley treats it as a matter of layering the right
capital and telling the right story. Johnson calls the stack "the most broken
part." The thesis belongs to the host.
- **Policy.** Johnson openly takes "the other side" of Sacca's post-policy
claim, arguing that on national-interest issues such as critical minerals the
government should and will act. Sacca says he hopes so but will not depend on
it.
- **"Apolitical" is about branding and dependence, not absence of views.** Sacca
has pointed views on environmental litigants, culture-war framing and a
politician he says voted against climate work and then attended a ribbon
cutting. The title's word describes his rule about not relying on government.
- **Unchallenged figures.** Antora's cost and size claims, Arbor's economics
and Lilac's extraction figures come from investors about companies they own,
the polling ranking has no named source, and the host tests none of them.
Panthalassa's 2 cents is a target.
- **Kelley's market-expansion case is hypothetical.** She argues power 5 or 10
times cheaper would open a much larger market of unknown size; no figure for
that market is given. Her one anchor, NextEra at "a $156 billion market cap"
as a measure of demand at today's prices, comes in a garbled sentence that does
not make the comparison recoverable.
- **The disclosure.** Lowercarbon is an investor in the host's company and holds
a board observer seat, and the host and guests praise each other warmly. It bears
most on the financing discussion, where Crux is in the same business.
