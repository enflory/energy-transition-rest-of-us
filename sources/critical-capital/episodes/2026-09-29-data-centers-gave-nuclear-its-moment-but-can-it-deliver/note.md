## The question

Data center demand and bipartisan support have given nuclear power an opening,
but can it actually be built at a cost that makes it worth building, and what
else does the clean build-out need that capital alone will not supply?

## The answer

Pierpoint's answer is that in the United States nuclear will rise and fall on
cost, and specifically on the cost of constructing a very large, complex
project, not necessarily on the reactor itself, and not on public acceptance.
The fix she points to is standardization and, ideally, serial building; she has
moved away from small modular reactors and thinks the country should "probably"
build big ones, and she argues project finance can only arrive after costs
become stable, not before. She does not say whether nuclear will deliver.
More broadly, she says plenty of capital exists for the transition but it wants
proven, low-risk projects, so first-of-a-kind technology needs government and
ideally philanthropic money, and the biggest item on her list for government
is restoring trust that its funding commitments will be honored.

## The argument

Pierpoint starts by refusing the premise that there is one problem to solve.
She lists several running at once (trouble in global oil markets, energy access
in the Global South, climate change) and says the solutions are equally
plural: the field has long treated climate as something to invent its way out
of, but some of the technologies now exist and the hard part has become
deployment, which needs government, industry, technology developers and
investors moving together. That framing matters for nuclear, because she
separates it from the story people expect. In Japan the obstacle after
Fukushima is public and political resistance. In the United States and many
other wealthy countries (she says "OECD countries") the obstacle is simply
cost, for existing plants as well as new ones, which is why there was an
extended period of closures. Public opinion has shifted toward nuclear,
largely because of climate change, but she says the American and probably European story will
still be decided by cost.

Her account of that cost is the core of the episode. For gas, the cost of
electricity is largely fuel; for nuclear it is almost entirely construction,
because once the plant exists the fuel, though not free, "almost might as
well be." And the
construction problem is not special to reactors. She recalls a study, from
perhaps 15 or 20 years ago, finding that about two-thirds of billion-dollar
megaprojects worldwide ran over budget and over schedule, and reads it as
evidence that humans are bad at building big, complex things, of which a
nuclear plant is one. So the lever is standardization and ideally building the
same design repeatedly, which she concedes is hard to do outside China. She
has also come off the small modular reactor idea, having been publicly on
several sides of it. Plants grew large for a reason: safety demands systems
and redundancy, and more output means more revenue to offset what they cost.
Small reactors bet on a different economy of scale, factory production and
shipping, but she says it has become clear that is not really possible: it
needs many orders of one design, still leaves substantial custom work on site,
and runs against buyers such as data centers who want large blocks of power.
Her conclusion is that "at this stage" the country should probably build big
reactors and work out how to cut their cost.

The host asks whether financing nuclear as a standalone project, rather than on
a utility's balance sheet, would itself lower costs. She says the causation
runs the other way. Project lenders need a clear, stable cost structure and a
likely revenue stream, and nuclear cannot yet offer the first; she cites the
Vogtle reactors at 200 percent over budget and similar experience in France. The
same logic generalizes to the whole transition. The large pool of institutional
infrastructure capital is built to price very low-risk, ideally contracted
projects. First-of-a-kind and even tenth-of-a-kind plants do not fit that, and
she says closing the gap needs substantial federal and, ideally, philanthropic
support. Hyperscalers "absolutely" have a role, but she warns against getting
hopes up too high: they too want projects on time and on budget, and for many
of them the overriding priority is as much power as possible, immediately,
which she says will always outrank a desire to innovate. She grants that they
know they have a public perception problem with data centers and sees an
opening for clean energy, though more for technologies close to what has been
built before than for the most novel. The one new sign she flags, which she
did not expect, is that hyperscalers are "potentially" willing to pay on
sliding scales where they see real benefit, or even competitive advantage, in
a technology.

Because that gap depends on government money, the damage she worries about
most is to trust in it. She says the list she took to the Senate still stands,
including boring items like fixing contracting, but that were she at the
Department of Energy today she would throw it out to rebuild what has been
damaged, starting with basic trust that a signed government funding contract
will be honored. The new sense that it may not be has, she says, spread
through the ecosystem, to the point that she hears venture investors describe portfolios as
"government proof." Her remedy is to "write the quiet parts down": codify what
spending and contracting mean, narrow the conditions for cancellation, and set
rules for programs that span administrations. She adds that none of this holds
unless courts enforce it.

## What you need to know first

- **Levelized cost of electricity.** A way of comparing technologies by
spreading a plant's full lifetime cost over the electricity it produces, giving
a cost per kilowatt-hour.
- **Standardization and serialization.** Building the same reactor design
repeatedly, ideally in sequence with the same workforce, so each project learns
from the last. Her main route to lower nuclear costs.
- **Small modular reactors.** Smaller reactors meant to be built in a factory
and shipped, trading the economies of size for economies of mass production.
- **Project finance versus balance-sheet finance.** Lending against a single
project's own costs and revenues, rather than funding it from a large
company's overall balance sheet. It requires the predictable costs nuclear has
not shown.

## Details worth keeping

- A nuclear company she advises reported community opposition to its project.
She assumed the concern was radiological; the chief executive said everyone was
fine with the reactor and the opposition was to the data center that would buy
its power. She attributes data center opposition to noise and appearance in
part, but says it is "probably" a broader fear of AI that mainly drives it.
- She expects a lot of gas to be built for data centers, and says it already
is, with that infrastructure locked in. She also sees the demand as an opening
for large clean base-load power such as nuclear.
- On AI and grid security she says she slept worst in her life while working on
infrastructure cybersecurity at the Department of Energy, and that this moment
is scarier, because the tools for disruption have become far easier for bad
actors to obtain in just the last couple of months. She wishes the country were
"dropping everything" to harden infrastructure.
- Her anecdote from a federal grid emergency exercise: one participant assumed
the public could be reached through hand-crank radios once cell towers failed,
which she took as a sign of untested assumptions built into the system.
- Asked which government does this well, she says she does not know of one
in particular, but points to nuclear: outside the United States it is mostly
run by state-sponsored utilities, and South Korea has kept costs stable as it
built more reactors, which she says can really only happen with something like
central planning. She limits the lesson to "certain pockets" and certain
technologies. She also cites FutureGen
among past Department of Energy megaprojects that went badly.
- The technology she has changed her mind about is AI. She is not yet ready to
say it will transform clean energy innovation, though she allows that fields
like materials development could be greatly helped.
- Her five-year picture of success, offered with doubt about feasibility: a
new Paris agreement with "teeth somehow"; companies moving together without
waiting for regulation; technologies such as geothermal and some cement processes crossing the threshold where
mainstream infrastructure investors build them at scale; and large amounts of
what she calls CDR infrastructure, a term the episode does not spell out.

## Claims worth citing

All figures as stated on 2026-09-29 and attributed to the speaker. Cost figures
and public-opinion findings move over time.

- Solar, storage and wind cost about a fifth to a tenth as much as nuclear per
kilowatt-hour, even after adjusting for subsidy. The host attributes this to
Lazard's annual levelized cost assessment; the phrasing of the subsidy
adjustment is loose. (Lazard, cited by Johnson)
- About two-thirds of all megaprojects globally, defined as costing $1 billion
or more, came in over budget and over schedule. She recalls the study from
roughly 15 to 20 years ago and does not name it. (unnamed study, cited by
Pierpoint)
- The Vogtle reactors in the United States ended up 200 percent over budget,
with similar experience in France. (Pierpoint)
- South Korea is one of the few places that have not seen costs balloon as more
reactors were built, keeping them stable and even lowering them slightly.

- In a survey the host saw, data centers ranked well below a methadone clinic
and a prison as something people would accept in their neighborhood. Source not
named. (Johnson)
- Cell towers have only 72 hours of backup power, as a participant stated in
the exercise she described. (Pierpoint, recounting a federal exercise)
- The host cites the Google-Fervo deal and Meta's deals with Oklo and TerraPower
as examples of hyperscalers backing new technologies. (Johnson)

## Where it's contested

The host does not challenge the guest. The two stated differences run the
other way: she rejects a proposition in one of his questions, and he adds
optimism she does not take up.

- **She reverses the host's project-finance premise.** He asks whether solving
nuclear's lack of project finance would bring costs down; she says it
"unfortunately has to be the other way around," with stable costs first.

- **The monologue is more bullish than the guest.** The host's opening says
nuclear looks set to be one of the biggest long-term winners. Pierpoint never
says that. She says nuclear can play an important role, opens her cost answer
with enthusiasm for building lots of solar and wind, and frames everything as
conditional on nuclear getting out of its own way on cost. The title asks
whether nuclear can deliver; the episode does not answer it.
- **Her outlook on the data center era is a hedged guess, and the host is more
optimistic.** Asked to look back from 20 years out, she says, as "a betting
person," that AI will be more efficient, less infrastructure will be needed
than expected, and some of it will be clean but far less than she would like.
The host replies that he is "weirdly optimistic," arguing that solar, wind and
storage are increasingly winning on the merits. That view is his; she does not
take it up.
- **She flags her own changes of mind.** She says she has been publicly on
multiple sides of small modular reactors, and presents the current position as
updated understanding rather than settled.
- **The government-design ideas are mostly the host's.** The resilience of tax
credits, a government-sponsored infrastructure bank insulated from changes of
administration, and In-Q-Tel as a model (relayed from a former Treasury
colleague) are Johnson's contributions. Pierpoint agrees with the infrastructure
bank idea and adds her own reason, the poor record of Department of Energy
megaprojects; she offers the Greenhouse Gas Reduction Fund as something that
had similar potential and "has not turned out as desired."
- **The guest's position.** Trellis funds first-of-a-kind projects in advanced
nuclear, geothermal and carbon removal, and she advises a nuclear company. Her
argument that such projects need catalytic public and philanthropic capital is
also a description of her own work, which she states openly.
- **What went unexamined.** No cost figure for a standardized large reactor, no
timeline, and no estimate of how much of the data center load nuclear could
serve appears in the episode.
