## The question

Can geothermal power get cheap the way shale oil and gas did, and what would it
take to build it far beyond Utah and Nevada?

## The answer

Jewett's answer is that key pieces of the shale playbook were easy to adopt,
chiefly one design repeated well after well and land leased before anyone
wanted it, and that Fervo has kept to off-the-shelf oilfield tools below
ground. Money is the hard part, and she thinks it still is, because a company
can typically finance only what it has already proven rather than what it
expects. She expects the $3,000-a-kilowatt goal to arrive through
many small improvements rather than one breakthrough, ties reach beyond Utah and
Nevada to that falling cost, and asks of permitting that it be predictable
enough for risk retired in one state to count in the next.

## The argument

Jewett's framing line is that Fervo runs the shale playbook twenty years later
with the answer key, and the lesson she takes from shale is specific. In every
basin an operator's first wells are its most expensive, and costs fall as
it learns the resource, spreads fixed infrastructure over more activity, and
eventually fights over minutes of nonproductive time. So Fervo built wellfield
plans to drill the same well over and over without redesign, and carried the
idea to the surface with near-identical power plants, each learning from the
last. She
calls these pieces of the shale strategy very easy to adopt. Below ground the
supply chain is close to oil and gas, differing mainly in higher temperatures
and often wider diameters, and Fervo has held to off-the-shelf tools rather
than inventing its own, which is why its development temperatures have risen
only as fast as existing equipment is rated for, with suppliers upsizing tools
and switching materials as they came to believe in the company. The land
strategy followed the same logic: on the conviction that the physics would
eventually work, the founding team leased the best geothermal acreage that also
had a believable route to a customer, while nobody else cared about it.

The intuitive story is that a pilot proves the concept and then drives the cost
curve, and when the host puts that premise to her, Jewett gently reframes it.
Fervo moved through deliberately escalating steps: desktop modeling, a
stimulation on an existing well, then its own well system, Project Red, which
showed that heat could be flowed and extracted. Fervo insisted Red be a
commercial pilot as well as a technical one, with a buyer (Google), because
doing the two in sequence would have been too many steps. But Red was a
minimum viable product, built to learn from and then repeat at scale rather
than being "really repeatable" in its own design, with only two wells alike.
There were large gains from its first well to its second, yet the
learning curve only really showed itself from the third well onward at Cape
Station, the large greenfield development Red green-lit. The curve needs
volume, and in a business she calls capital-intensive, volume needs money.

That is the turn in her account: financing has been the hardest thing for
Fervo, and she thinks it remains so. You skate to where the puck is going, but
you can typically finance only where you have got to. At the front of a
technology cycle the cost of capital stays high however creative you are,
because there is no operating record, the technology is not yet proven to
work, and, at Red, there was no full-scale fifteen-year power purchase
agreement with a creditworthy buyer. The way down
is to announce milestones and expected results in advance, come back with
results that match, and let each data point widen the financing available; she
calls this financing 101, applying in spades to a technology perceived as
high-risk. Two outside factors helped, arriving at the same moment: the
Inflation Reduction Act tax credit, which she calls incredibly helpful, and
rising demand and willingness to pay for geothermal in 2023 and 2024.
Together, she says, they convinced people the projects would be financially
healthy, and she thinks that helped attract more traditional financing.

Demand and geography close the argument. Jewett rejects the idea that the
business is alive only because of hyperscalers. She joined convinced of a large
market before AI was on her radar, driven by rolling blackouts, renewable
portfolio standard targets and the retirement of nuclear and older fossil
plants, even without meaningful load growth. AI added to that demand in a
meaningful way and made it more obvious; on her account it did not create it.
Geographic reach, which she calls the reason Fervo exists, she ties to cost:
geothermal could be a global resource because its cost is coming down enough to
compete in many places. What she wants from permitting reform is
predictability, so that risk pulled out through repeated proof points in Utah
carries into Nevada, New Mexico or Colorado instead of resetting the risk
profile and the financing playbook in each new state, which would undo the
track record her financing argument depends on. Nor does she expect one
breakthrough to reach the cost goal: it will come little by little, from small,
human-led improvements, an answer she concedes disappoints people asking for
the big unlock.

## What you need to know first

- **Enhanced geothermal.** Conventional geothermal depends on hot water or steam
already trapped in the rock. Enhanced geothermal creates the reservoir by
fracturing hot, dry rock and circulating water through it. That description
comes from the host's monologue; Jewett says only that Fervo's wells were
drilled and fractured so heat could be flowed and extracted.
- **Learning curve.** The fall in cost that comes from repeating the same job:
each identical well or plant gets cheaper as the operator learns the ground,
shares fixed equipment and cuts idle time.
- **Power purchase agreement, or offtake.** A contract in which a buyer commits
to purchase a project's output. Jewett contrasts the "creative" offtake signed
for the pilot with the full-scale, long-term kind that persuades financiers.
- **Cost per kilowatt.** A cost measured against generating capacity, not
against electricity delivered.

## Details worth keeping

- Jewett first tried oil and gas as a final college internship, to understand
"the dark side of energy," then joined a traveling Schlumberger fracturing crew
out of Rock Springs, Wyoming. After an MBA she returned to oilfield services
before finding subsurface clean-energy companies.
- Project Red's build: after a four-stage stimulation on an existing, highly
deviated well, a planned two-well system at the same site ended up as three
wells, two horizontal and one vertical for monitoring.
- Cape Station on the ground: rows of multi-well pads, power plants built to
look alike, and a transmission line with Fervo's own substation to the grid.
She calls multi-well pads a Fervo first in geothermal, replacing the single
vertical well per pad used before. A data center, by contrast, she describes as
a series of HVAC-equipped warehouses.
- Fervo expects to deliver power three ways: existing transmission, new
transmission, and direct connection to a customer's load behind the meter. She
welcomes the bipartisan Senate proposal for more predictable in-state and
interstate transmission, and names a bill the transcript renders as "BAAJA,"
not spelled out, as the kind of measure that would make federal permitting
consistent across states.
- On geothermal's bipartisan appeal she declines the credit: the story sells
itself, as a shale-adjacent technology that redeploys the shale workforce,
tools and innovation and replaces fossil fuel one for one.
- In 2023 she expected Fervo to be selling to Google in a meaningful way by
2026, but did not "necessarily" expect to be talking about a specific
potential co-located development as the buyer.
- Being public has not changed the day-to-day. The cost is constant market
feedback that, it feels to her, does not always track operational milestones;
beyond the capital raised, the benefit is a much larger audience learning
about geothermal.

## Where it's contested

The host challenges nothing, but the guest quietly departs from his framing in
several places, and those divergences are the main content here.

- **AI is not the premise.** The introduction says Fervo has diversified its
customers beyond the AI boom. Jewett's order is the reverse: she calls
hyperscaler dependence a misconception and says the demand case came first.
- **Project Red was not the cost-curve step.** The host's question presumes it
was; her reframing is in the argument above.
- **No one places a data center at Cape.** Asked to picture a large geothermal
project near an AI data center, she sketches a data center only generically
and describes Cape Station's pads, plants and grid connection. The host's recap
joins the two sketches into one scene. Neither says a data center sits at Cape,
and she calls co-location a "potential" development.
- **Transmission.** The host frames the problem as getting lines to Fervo's
public land, and the Senate proposal as easing transmission on public lands.
Jewett says existing transmission was always the plan and values the proposal
for predictability; she does not mention public lands.
- **The central claim is unquantified.** The learning curve is the episode's
spine, yet no per-well cost, drilling time, current cost per kilowatt or
electricity price appears, so the $3,000 goal has no baseline. That drilling
times and costs have plummeted is the monologue's claim, not hers.
- **Her own hedges.** Geothermal "could" be a global resource, and cutting
enough cost would make it "potentially" the cheapest electricity on Earth; she
says the ambition feels crazy and happily accepts being called "the crazy
geothermal lady."
- **A question nobody asked.** The host remarks in passing that federal tax law
has been changing a lot, then asks why geothermal has stayed above the
political fray. No one asks whether geothermal's own credit has changed, so the
episode's financing story rests on the credit as she describes it.
- **The guest's stake.** Jewett is an executive of the newly public company
whose plan the episode describes. Its forward figures, from the cost goal to
the many plants planned at Cape, are company targets rather than results.
