## The question

How did Intersect build a power developer that Alphabet would buy whole, and
what does its founder think the power system serving large loads will look like?

## The answer

Kimber's account is that Intersect made two separate, deliberate bets against
the consensus: that long-term power contracts at the prices then on offer
destroyed value, so leaving output uncontracted was the better risk-adjusted
choice; and that large loads would have to move to places with abundant clean
energy plus enough gas to firm it. What the buyer paid for, as he tells it, was
the growth machine and the development pipeline built on the second bet. Looking
forward, he expects a self-reinforcing cycle of renewables, batteries and cheap,
flexible gas to shape the system for the foreseeable future, and to crowd out a
lot of next-generation technologies.

## The argument

The opening monologue tells this as one story: Intersect went merchant, built
before it had buyers, and was rewarded when AI demand arrived and it could offer
power ready to deliver immediately. Kimber tells it as two bets with two
different rationales. One was the data-center thesis, written into the original
business plan: that there was a great deal of energy in the wrong place at the
wrong time, the grid was underinvested, and large loads would therefore come to
where the generation was. The other was arithmetic about contracts. In the 2016
to 2019 period, he says, developers were signing power purchase agreements at
around $20 per megawatt-hour, betting on capital cost declines that had stopped
happening, which locked in no developer profit from day one. Intersect compared
a contracted project against one that simply rode the projected market price
with that price curve cut in half, and found the uncontracted project still came
out ahead. He is emphatic that this was a calculation about that market, not a
creed: he says people wrongly take him for a proponent of merchant power, and
that today he would sign contracts, because they now pay much more.

The decision framework he applies to these choices is risk, return and
liquidity, and the way he applies it turns against the intuitive reading. Buying
panels and transformers before projects had buyers looks reckless project by
project: the equipment backed projects returning roughly 12 to 14 percent on a
leveraged basis, financed with private equity money he says was probably priced
above that. The justification had to come from elsewhere, and it came from
speed. Building at that pace earned the company a growth premium on its
valuation, so the platform was worth more than its projects, and he reads the
exit as proof: the buyer valued the machine, not only what it had made. The same
logic explains choices that sound like temperament. Intersect took growth
capital rather than infrastructure capital, ran a small team on a few very large
projects, and killed weak projects early rather than fighting communities or
complicated tax credits to the finish, which he calls a testament to endurance
rather than to building a scalable business.

The merchant bet's record is mixed in his own telling. The first portfolio,
financed in a deal he calls the hardest of his life, had a strong first couple
of years and then a poor couple of years, even though contracted power prices
are very high. When the host suggests batteries are flattening the spot market,
Kimber treats that as one cause among several. He also blames regulators and
market operators for practices that damp spot-price volatility, which he likens
to a hidden capacity market in which consumers are probably paying more
elsewhere, plus incentives that push utilities to bid down solar prices at
summer peaks, and an overbuild of midday solar and batteries. His warning is
that muting price signals borrows from the future, because firming resources
like batteries and new gas need those signals to get built.

His view of the future rests on a distinction between energy and capacity. Wind
and solar are the cheapest energy available when they are running, but they are
not the cheapest capacity, so they need firming. Getting to 100 percent
renewables would take truly massive cost changes, and people will not tolerate
an unreliable grid, so he sees a lasting role for gas machines even if they
rarely run. Cheaper renewables made batteries more valuable, and more batteries
let more renewables onto the grid, which drives renewable costs down again. Add
low-capital-cost gas generators as a third point on that wheel, inefficient
perhaps, short-lived, modular, and the cycle strengthens. He argues any new
technology, nuclear and fusion included, will have to compete against this
combination, which he frames as betting against battery storage. The corollary
is that the intermittency of renewables is migrating into the gas system: he
expects value to shift toward gas storage and pipelines that can deliver fuel
fast rather than continuously, and he wonders aloud whether buyers will pay for
the option to burn gas rather than the gas itself. He prefaces this by saying he
is not a gas expert.

## What you need to know first

- **Merchant versus contracted.** A contracted plant sells its output in advance
  under a long-term power purchase agreement at a fixed price. A merchant plant
  sells into the market at whatever the price turns out to be, taking the risk
  and the upside.
- **Behind the meter, "bring your own power."** Generation built alongside the
  load it serves, often off the grid entirely, so a data center supplies its own
  electricity rather than drawing from the shared system.
- **Energy versus capacity.** Energy is the electricity actually delivered.
  Capacity is the ability to deliver it on demand, especially at the hardest
  hours. Kimber's whole forward argument depends on keeping these apart.
- **Firming.** Pairing an intermittent source with something that can be
  dispatched at will, so the combination can be counted on.

## Details worth keeping

- Kimber's founding lesson from teaching project finance is what he calls the
  conservation of risk: structured finance rarely eliminates risk, it only
  bounds, moves and prices it.
- His stated reason for leaving the conventional model was that it had become a
  repetitive routine (cheapest contract, cheapest Chinese panels, sell to a
  pension fund, repeat) that was running out of steam, required no insight about
  the grid's future, and in some cases was not good for the grid.
- Siting starts from strategy rather than land: a thesis about a product is
  turned into map layers of gas, good wind and good solar, which produce a heat
  map of candidate places. Intersect starts from raw land and very rarely buys
  other developers' early projects.
- His checklist for data centers done right: transparency with the community,
  bring your own power, dry cooling and minimal water use, noise, light and
  traffic abatement, and a community-directed benefit fund such as endowments
  with local foundations. He says he feels claims about data-center water use
  are the biggest outright lies on social media.
- He says the data-center backlash does not make much sense to him and that it
  has been over-politicized and manipulated. Nobody in the episode tests this.
- On why the buyer acquired the company, he guesses that it had been planning
  two or three project deals with Intersect and concluded buying the company
  would cost about the same. He also says it is one of the few buyers he would
  have sold to, citing cultural fit and how tightly the two can now integrate
  data center and generation.
- The host likens Intersect's plants to a plug-in hybrid car. Kimber calls them
  off-grid "EREV" power plants, glossed on air only as an electric vehicle with a
  "kicker" gas motor, and says the power electronics and microgrid techniques
  developed behind the meter will later be rolled onto the grid.
- He floats, as a possibility rather than a prediction, that data centers could
  end up making the gas system more flexible in ways that benefit the grid
  serving ordinary consumers.

## Where it's contested

There is no real disagreement in this episode. The host builds on every answer,
so the useful content is where framing and substance diverge and what went
unexamined.

- **The monologue merges two bets the guest keeps apart** (see the argument).
  It also omits that the early merchant assets have had a poor couple of years,
  which its "huge payoff" framing does not reflect.
- **Batteries and the spot market.** The host offers batteries as the reason
  spot prices have flattened. Kimber treats it as one factor among several,
  alongside regulatory practice and policy incentives.
- **The exit as validation.** Kimber cites the sale as evidence for his thesis
  and his growth-premium reasoning. That is the founder's reading of his own
  outcome, and the episode offers no other.
- **Hedges on the gas argument.** He disclaims gas expertise and says his
  forecast of more gas storage and faster pipelines comes from conversations
  he has had; paying for options on fuel he poses only as a question. The
  claim that renewables plus batteries is "unstoppable" is stated without
  hedging and without numbers.
- **An unexplained mechanism.** In the spot-market answer he blames policies
  rendered "RA policy," "capacity policy" and "RECs" for perverse incentives,
  without explaining how they work. Neither acronym is spelled out on air.
- **Transcript quirk.** One paragraph is labeled with the speaker "Water"; it is
  Kimber continuing his checklist, with the item name rendered as a speaker
  label.
- **The disclosure.** Kimber is an early investor in Crux, stated by the host at
  the close; Kimber's reply is consistent with it.
