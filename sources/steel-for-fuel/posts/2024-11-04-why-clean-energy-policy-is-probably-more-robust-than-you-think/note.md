## The question

Is US clean energy policy as easy to unwind as an election result suggests?

## The answer

Probably not. No top state for the manufacturing money is reliably Democratic,
and Lubershane reads a letter from eighteen Republican members of Congress as
evidence that this is already shaping their incentives.

## The argument

The post is one chart and a few notes on it, published in US election week.
The chart tracks dollars actually committed to clean energy projects, and this
note cannot reproduce it: the state rankings the argument rests on sit in the
image. Their shape is the whole case. None of the leading states for clean
energy manufacturing money is reliably Democratic and half of the top ten are
Republican, forming what he calls a battery belt from the industrial midwest
into the southeast. Changing the law would therefore threaten investment in
Republican states, which is why he found it unsurprising that eighteen
Republican members had already written to the Speaker urging caution and, in
the letter's phrase, "business and market certainty".

## What you need to know first

- **Battery belt.** His name for the corridor of clean energy factories the
Inflation Reduction Act is seeding, from the industrial midwest into the
southeast.
- **Clean Energy Investment Monitor.** A Rhodium Group and MIT tracker of real
dollars committed to US clean energy projects, and the chart's source.

## Details worth keeping

- He singles out Georgia, crediting a decade of state economic development well
beyond clean energy.
- He names Southern Company there as Energy Impact Partners' founding partner,
and the firm's Atlanta office.
- One note opens "Zilch. Nada.", answering a question the text never states.

## Claims worth citing

All figures as stated on 2024-11-04.

- None of the top states for clean energy manufacturing dollars is reliably
Democratic, and half of the top ten are Republican. (Lubershane, reading the
Clean Energy Investment Monitor)
- Eighteen Republican members of Congress wrote to Speaker Mike Johnson in
August urging caution over changes to the Inflation Reduction Act.
(Lubershane)

## Where it's contested

Nobody argues back. The "probably" in the title is doing real work: he never
says repeal is off the table, only that the geography of the money makes it
harder, and he flags the political reading as a rare injection of punditry.
His one stated interest sits in that Georgia credit.
