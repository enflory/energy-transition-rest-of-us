## The question

Is the long-awaited return of nuclear power actually close, and can the West
relearn how to build reactors cheaply and quickly enough for it to count?

## The answer

A good chance, but not a done deal. Of the three conditions Lubershane sets,
public support and government support are "absolutely" being regained; the
third, an industry set up to build cheaply and quickly, is only "mostly" on
track. The Western industry is beginning to follow the standardize-and-repeat
playbook that worked in China and Korea, but he worries, especially about
America, that limited resources will be spread across too many new reactor
technologies, and he says it will take at least a decade to know.

## The argument

Beyond the familiar case that nuclear is clean and firm (available whenever
called on, unlike solar and wind), he argues it has four underrated virtues: it
is dense, using less land over its life cycle than any other source; scalable,
deployable almost anywhere with ample fuel; safe; and secure, far less exposed
to weather, trade wars and real wars. The safety case is made mostly by quoting
four paragraphs of his own earlier, unnamed writing, which this post uses as
support rather than revising. Against that stand two potential deal breakers,
"big ones": across what he calls "the entire Western hemisphere", with few
exceptions, nuclear has been extremely expensive and slow to build since the
early 1980s. His history of why spreads the cause. Capacity was on track to
keep growing exponentially when Three Mile Island in 1979 and Chernobyl in 1986
turned public opinion in North America and Europe sharply against nuclear. (He
invites a comparison of nuclear's early growth with solar's adoption curve; two
uncaptioned figures accompany this history and the note cannot see them.) Some
states then effectively banned new plants, and the federal regulator imposed
new requirements on dozens already under construction, individually
well-intentioned, he says, but in aggregate the cause of years of rework.
Interest rates were high through the same years, and nuclear's heavy upfront
cost and long build make it especially sensitive to the cost of capital. Having
stopped, the West began to lose the capacity to build even if it wanted to, as
supply chains, experienced workers and process knowledge eroded; the US kept
some expertise through its nuclear navy, though naval reactors differ in design
and fuel.

The one serious American attempt at a restart this century, twin Westinghouse
AP-1000 projects begun in 2009, became the cautionary tale: V.C. Summer was
abandoned after Westinghouse went bankrupt, and Vogtle finished in 2024 at
about $15,000 per kilowatt. He argues Vogtle was unrepresentative, a case study
in Murphy's Law for a first-of-a-kind project, and that what went wrong "most
importantly" was a design not finished before ground was broken, which brought
years of back-and-forth with the regulator. Crucially, very little of it was
nuclear-specific: most of it could befall any first attempt at something new
and complicated that its regulator had not seen, though "nuclear being nuclear"
tends to accentuate such problems and adds an extra zero to the bill. The
remedy is the learning curve every other industry relies on, which needs a
second and third project to follow while the people who learned on the first
are still available. China and Korea are his evidence that it works for
nuclear, building this century for less than a third of Vogtle's cost (the
comparison is in a chart), and Russia by all accounts has done the same.

What those countries share is a formula: a few standardized designs, built
serially for decades, by concentrated institutions, with domestic supply chains
for critical components, all resting on long-term strategic commitment and
predictable regulation. New technology is not in it. A self-described
technology guy, he still judges the vast majority of emerging reactor
technology irrelevant to a renaissance within 20 years: first because most
next-generation designs need a fuel with no meaningful supply outside Russia
and China, and then because the need for standardization gives the industry
some natural monopolistic tendencies. From the second he concludes that a crowd
of reactor startups could do harm, by distracting the stakeholders who shape
procurement from the proven path; the largest data center companies, for
instance, have each gravitated to their own preferred Gen IV reactor company
rather than backing one.

That sets his three conditions: public support, government support, and an
industry able to make nuclear reliably cheap and fast enough to matter. Cheap
enough varies by region, but forced to choose one figure he offers $100 per
megawatt-hour as the line under which nuclear becomes attractive worldwide;
fast enough probably means under ten years from inception to power, with five
an ambitious goal. Nuclear is regaining the
first two, "absolutely", across many countries and both US parties, with the
public-opinion evidence carried largely in poll charts. The third is mostly on
track. A federal loan package for up to ten AP-1000s concentrates resources on
one proven design, which he calls the ideal role for government at this stage,
and there seems to be momentum behind GE-Hitachi's BWRX-300, a relatively
conservative small reactor. But he still worries that investors and the tech
industry can be too easily distracted by "shiny (theoretical) objects". He also
flags a setback himself: North America's first small modular reactor project, a
BWRX-300 under construction in Ontario, is running over budget and behind
schedule, which he calls very concerning.

## What you need to know first

- **First of a kind, and the learning curve.** The first project built to a
new design usually costs the most; later ones get cheaper as the same
contractors, crews and suppliers repeat it. The argument turns on reading
Vogtle as a first case rather than a typical one.
- **Capital cost versus cost of energy.** A cost per kilowatt is what it takes
to build each unit of a plant's capacity; a cost per megawatt-hour is the cost
of its electricity once construction, financing and operation are spread over
its output. Vogtle is quoted the first way, his $100 threshold the second.
- **Reactor generations.** His quoted earlier writing says Gen II reactors
(built last century) need active operator intervention when something fails,
while Gen III+ designs are intended to cool the core passively, and places Gen
IV in research and development. The present post calls the AP-1000 a Gen III+
design and reports construction permits for two Gen IV units. A small modular
reactor has more of itself built in a factory; the 300-megawatt BWRX-300 is
one, but a light water design on conventional fuel, which is why he calls it
conservative.
- **HALEU.** The post's name, never spelled out, for the fuel most Gen IV
designs need, which it describes as a form of highly enriched uranium.

## Details worth keeping

- The opening epigraph, credited to Leonardo DaVinci with a footnote asking
"Or was it DiCaprio?", is a joke that sets up the closing section on winners.
- The unfinished design's back-and-forth included a regulator mandate to
redesign the shield wall against aircraft strikes. Beyond it, Vogtle suffered
steel modules with some welds and fittings below nuclear standards, crews that
practically all lacked significant nuclear experience, and contractor collapse
that left Southern Company managing the project.
- He roots Chinese and Korean political will in treating oil and gas import
dependence as an existential risk. Korea has kept one reactor platform since
the 1980s; China has standardized on three. He believes their concentrated
operators reflect nuclear's complexity and how few projects one person sees in
a career.
- He frames policy as starting either from harnessing nuclear and asking how to
minimize risk, or from avoiding nearly all risk and asking whether nuclear is
still an option. The US and many European countries have lived under the
second since the 1980s; the first "might be sufficient". Government is involved
regardless, because commercial fuel is linked to weapons fuel, and only it can
value benefits like security that markets undervalue.
- Beyond the poll charts, his public-opinion evidence is his own observation
that the environmental movement's anti-nuclear "old guard" has given way to
pragmatic carbon hawks, and a Berkeley club poster. He ties Europe's shift to
Russia's attack on Ukraine and the cutoff of Russian gas. Japan's polls predate
the US attacks on Iran and the closure of the Strait of Hormuz, which sent East
Asian oil and gas prices soaring (gas prices are in a chart); he bets the next
polls show more support.
- His closing section asks who wins if this is a renaissance, prefaced "not
investment advice". Korea "ought to" punch far above its weight as an
exporter; Gen III vendors gain (the AP-1000, the BWRX-300 and, "sadly",
Russia's VVER-1200, with Korea's APR1400 and the latest French design, EDF's
EPR2, seeming strong contenders), all but one gigawatt-class; so does the fuel
supply chain, given dependence on Russia for enrichment, with a shout-out to
DISA Uranium for recovering uranium long treated as mining waste; and so do
incumbent component makers such as Doosan, Japan Steel Works, Framatome and
Curtiss-Wright, which he expects to face little new competition.

## Claims worth citing

All figures as stated on 2026-11-18. Project, permit and funding figures were
moving quickly when written.

- Nuclear supplies about 10% of global electricity, about 2% of final energy
demand. (Lubershane)
- Solar photovoltaics need more than fifty times as much land as nuclear for
the same zero-emissions electricity, on a full life-cycle basis including fuel.
(Lubershane, with a chart from Our World in Data using UNECE data)
- The world has about 8 million tons of identified uranium resources, about
120 years at current consumption; he adds that seawater uranium could probably
be extracted at a tolerable cost if necessary. (World Nuclear Association,
cited by Lubershane)
- The world commissioned over 100 gigawatts of nuclear capacity in the 1970s;
China now has more under construction than the rest of the world combined.
(Lubershane, the second with a World Nuclear Association chart)
- Ten US states effectively banned new construction after the accidents; since
2020, five states have overturned longstanding prohibitions. (Lubershane)
- On practically any measure, fission's death toll is lower than that of every
other major energy source except solar, and Chernobyl was the one case of a
nuclear incident directly killing people. (Lubershane's earlier writing, quoted
in this post) A present-day footnote adds that all recorded deaths related to
Fukushima came from the tsunami rather than radiation, though the radiation
release forced a major evacuation that caused some deaths among sick and
elderly residents and displaced tens of thousands for an extended period.
(Lubershane)
- All the spent fuel ever generated in the US could theoretically fit on one
football field in casks roughly ten feet tall. (Lubershane's earlier writing,
quoted in this post)
- Vogtle's two units total 2.2 gigawatts, came online fifteen years after
construction began, and overran schedule and budget by more than 2X. It had a
$12 billion loan guarantee and peaked at about 9,000 workers on site.
(Lubershane)
- Its capital cost is about six times a gas combined-cycle plant's even at record
turbine prices, and more than three times solar with twelve hours of
batteries, assuming $1,200 per kilowatt for solar and $300 per kilowatt-hour
for storage. Depending on financing it implies roughly $200 per megawatt-hour,
the most expensive generation practically anywhere. (Lubershane)
- Vogtle's first major outlays came fourteen years before it earned revenue.
(Lubershane) Financing added the equivalent of about $3,400 per kilowatt in
"overnight" capital cost, more than the whole capital cost of a new gas
combined-cycle plant. (Georgia Power, cited by Lubershane; the comparison is
his)
- Chinese and Korean plants sometimes come in below a fifth of Vogtle's cost,
and most are finished in under ten years. (Lubershane; his chart cites Liu et
al. in Nature, July 2025, and Georgia Power filings)
- Korea's four APR1400 units in the United Arab Emirates cost about $4,500 "per
kilowatt-hour" as written, though every other nuclear capital cost in the post
is per kilowatt. (Lubershane)
- Russia's VVER-1200 has been exported to "over half a dozen" countries (he
names six) at reported capital costs of about $4,000 to $6,000 per kilowatt.
(Lubershane)
- The US has no forge capable of making heavy reactor pressure vessels.
(Lubershane)
- Japan shut down all 54 reactors after Fukushima; a majority now supports
restarting all of them, and restarts have begun. (Lubershane; the opinion data
is in a chart citing Asahi Shimbun and the World Nuclear Association)
- The regulator has issued construction permits for three commercial small
reactors (a GE-Hitachi BWRX-300 and a Kairos Gen IV unit in Tennessee, a
Terrapower Gen IV unit in Wyoming), each in under two years from application,
the latest in 14 months. An Oklo Gen IV reactor holds a Department of Energy
demonstration permit, with site work under way at Idaho National Labs.
(Lubershane)
- The Department of Energy's $17.5 billion loan package covers up to ten
AP-1000s at five sites, 11 gigawatts or a little over 10% of the existing US
fleet. A separate Commerce Department agreement with Korea's government, the
Korea Electric Power Corporation (KEPCO) and Westinghouse covers eight units:
six AP-1000s and two APR-1400s. (Lubershane)
- The Department of Energy awarded $2.7 billion to three companies to expand
domestic uranium enrichment, two of them focused on HALEU. (Lubershane)
- European governments have made a rhetorical shift, and "a few" are making
real commitments. This year the President of the European Commission and
Germany's Chancellor (named in the post as Mertz) both called abandoning nuclear a
"strategic mistake"; France and Sweden have begun offering subsidized loans and
above-market power contracts at €100 per megawatt-hour. (Lubershane)
- Thirteen units based on the licensed AP-1000 design are under construction
in China, and six AP-1000 projects are in advanced planning in Eastern Europe
(three in Poland, two in Bulgaria, one in Slovakia); Santee Cooper is taking
steps to finish the two abandoned Summer units. (Lubershane)
- Elementl Power plans a 1.5-gigawatt Ohio site using GE-Hitachi technology,
has a development agreement with Google, and has a preliminary $750 million
development capital agreement with a Korean investment consortium. (Elementl
Power's plans, reported by Lubershane)
- Three mothballed US reactors, one at Three Mile Island, are under contract
to restart with Google and Microsoft buying the output; over a dozen uprates
will add about 2% to US fleet capacity. (Lubershane)
- Sixteen fission startups have raised over $100 million in the past five
years and nine over $500 million; seventeen fusion companies have raised over
$100 million. (Lubershane's own count)

## Where it's contested

A solo essay with no second voice, so nothing is disputed in the post. What it
carries instead is a hedged conclusion and evidence against parts of it, mostly
supplied by the author himself.

- **The small reactor he highlights has a warning sign.** The Ontario overrun is
the post's only cost or schedule report from a project it describes as now
under construction, and he does not say what it means for his view that the
BWRX-300 "may turn out to be" the right size.
- **The thresholds are set but not tested.** Having defined success in cost and
years, he scores the industry on commitments (loans, permits, agreements), not
on whether any project is on course to meet either. His own conclusion defers
the verdict at least a decade.
- **The formula needs decades of commitment.** He draws it from countries where
he roots political will in fear of fuel imports; his US evidence is current
bipartisan support across two administrations. Durability is assumed, not
argued.
- **Startups are a risk, not a verdict.** He says they "could" be detrimental,
expects Gen IV to matter in the long run, and wants the best Gen IV designs
(Oklo, Kairos and Terrapower are his examples) proven and grown; his worry is
spreading limited resources now.
- **Uranium supply.** After citing about 120 years of identified resources, the
closing section says the world is "already short" on primary uranium supply,
with the evidence in a chart. The post does not connect the two, though it
frames the later problem as timing new capacity to new demand.
- **What he has at stake.** Elementl Power, cited as momentum for the
BWRX-300, is identified in the post as an EIP portfolio company; Energy Impact
Partners is the author's firm.
